Before We Fix the Child

Introduction

The world of education is a wonderful world, and it fascinates me from several directions: as a father, as a teacher and educator, and also as a person who is trying to understand himself.

Over the years I have learned that working with children does not only bring me face to face with the children I raise and teach. Again and again, it brings me face to face with myself. The more I try to understand them, the more I am asked to look inward as well, at what is happening inside me.

At the start of Yirmiyahu's mission, Hashem says to him:

״רְאֵה הִפְקַדְתִּיךָ הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה עַל הַגּוֹיִם וְעַל הַמַּמְלָכוֹת, לִנְתוֹשׁ וְלִנְתוֹץ וּלְהַאֲבִיד וְלַהֲרוֹס, לִבְנוֹת וְלִנְטוֹעַ״

“See, I have set you this day over the nations and over the kingdoms, to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant.”

(Yirmiyahu 1:10).

I think of this verse because there is something in it that touches every parent and every teacher deeply.

Our children and our students are deeply affected by us: by the words we say to them, by the way we respond to them, by the way we look at them, by the trust we give them, and also by the boundaries we set.

In a certain sense, they really are ״כחומר ביד היוצר״, “like clay in the potter's hand,” an image that also appears later in Sefer Yirmiyahu. We take part in shaping the way they will see themselves and the world around them, sometimes for the rest of their lives.

That is an enormous responsibility.

With a single word it is possible to uproot and tear down something inside a child, sometimes without even meaning to. And with a word, a look, patience and trust, it is possible to build and to plant.

That is why raising children asks us not only to ask what the child needs to do differently, but also to stop and look at what we are doing, where we are acting from, and what kind of mark we hope to leave on him.

In the end, the great question for us as parents and teachers is how to be, as much as we possibly can, on the side of ״לבנות ולנטוע״, “to build and to plant.”

That is what all of us long for.

Two people have influenced me greatly in the field of education, a field which, as I mentioned, also touches my own life and my inner world. I call them my primary teachers:

Rabbi Binyamin Gottlieb
and parenting guide Michal Berenham.

Rabbi Binyamin Gottlieb taught me the secret of authority.

The secret of authority begins with our own inner certainty as parents. When I am sure of my place, I am not looking to the child for approval, agreement or justification for what I asked. I also do not need to threaten, punish or explain again and again.

I asked. You heard. Now do it.

Not out of anger, not out of a need for power and not out of an argument, but out of a simple, clear knowing: I am the parent, and when I ask my child to do something, he is supposed to listen to me.

When this certainty truly lives inside us, the child almost always feels it too. The surer we are of our authority, the less force we need to keep it.

That is the secret of authority.

Years later, Michal Berenham's teaching, called “Emotional Parenting,” entered this same place, naturally and with great power.

I have learned an enormous amount from Michal Berenham. Her teaching truly changed my life. It is a wide, deep sea of insights, fine distinctions, inner work, and above all practical tools that help me understand what is happening inside me, what is happening inside the child, and how to act from a more accurate place.

It is hard to sum all of this up in a few sentences, but if I try to touch the heart of it in one: when I come to educate a child, I first need to look at myself, meet what is happening inside me, and understand where I am educating from.

Am I triggered? Have I made room for my feelings and agreed to accept them? Can I stay with the child even in the face of feelings that are hard for me to meet, without rushing to silence them or solve them?

And the question of all questions: am I able to love the child not as a personal need that he meets for me, but with a love that does not depend on anything, even when he does things I do not like, and even when he does not meet the need that is stirring inside me?

Alongside the fine distinctions and the inner work that Michal brings to the world of education, her teaching also has an important foundation that I myself sometimes tend to emphasize less: self-compassion.

Self-compassion means understanding that every parent has better times and worse times, and that even if we got it wrong, it is okay. We make the effort, we try and we get it wrong, we repair, and sometimes we get it wrong again. The goal is not to be a perfect parent, but a parent who tries, learns and repairs.

This book is written in my own voice, out of my experience as a father and as a teacher. To me it also matters that this is the voice of a father who wants to talk not only about authority, boundaries and education, but also about fear, shame, helplessness and accepting feelings. I discovered that meeting the emotional world does not weaken my place as a father. On the contrary, it allows me to be a steadier, more connected and more accurate father.

The book rests on what I learned from the teachers I mentioned, as I understood them and applied them, alongside insights and tools I added from my own experience. It does not claim to represent any one method precisely, or the words of any particular person, and the responsibility for the interpretation and for what is written here is mine.

Some of the ideas in this book may be new, and some of the directions surprising and not always accepted. At times they may even cause an initial resistance. These pieces were originally written briefly, as short posts, so they do not always include a full, detailed explanation. Still, the principles come back throughout the book from different angles and in different contexts, and I believe that through this repetition they will become clearer and settle more and more in the heart.

I thank the parents who read these words along the way and responded to them. Through your responses I understood that the personal thoughts I share also reach other parents. That understanding gave me the desire to gather them into a book. My thanks go to you.

My beloved children and my dear students, you are my best coaches. Day after day you push me to sharpen myself, to look at things anew, and to try to really live them.

And just between us, you know better than anyone that even when I try to live these things, I do not always manage to apply them fully. I also get it wrong, repair and try again.

Everything written here was born out of my time with you.

Part I: What Happens Inside Me Before I React

Which feeling am I trying not to meet?

Chapter 1: Education, or Running from a Feeling?

The child does something we do not like.

He does not listen.
He is rude.
He hits.
He argues.

And sometimes he is not doing anything wrong at all. He is simply embarrassed, ashamed or holding back, but it is hard for us to see him like that.

Each of these behaviors stirs up unpleasant feelings in us, and sometimes even painful ones.

Worry:

“What will become of him?”
“How will he manage like this?”
“When will this finally pass?”

Shame:

“What do people think of me?”
“This is my child…”

Anger.
Frustration.
Helplessness.

And right away an urge rises in us to stop the behavior.

It seems to us that what bothers us is what the child is doing. But very often, what we are really trying to stop is the feeling his behavior stirs up in us.

We want the child to stop, so that we can stop feeling.

If he listens, we will not have to meet the helplessness that rises in us when we cannot lead him. If he stops bothering others, we will not have to meet the worry that rises in us: “What will become of him?” And if he would only stop being shy, we will not have to meet how hard it is for us to see him like that.

And so we try to solve what is happening inside us by changing the child, right now.

We rush:
to demand that he listen.
to stop the bothering.
to convince him he has no reason to be embarrassed.
to get angry.
to threaten.
to fix.

The main thing is that the behavior stops, and with it the helplessness, the worry or the discomfort that rose in us.

But before we set out to educate the child, we need to stop for a moment and agree to meet what is going on inside us.

Accepting the feeling

Accepting the feeling begins when I agree to feel what is already in me, without trying to get rid of it right away.

To stop means to turn my gaze for a moment from the child to myself and ask:

“What am I feeling right now?”

And to try to give the feeling a name:

I am angry right now. I am worried. I am embarrassed. I feel helpless.

I do not need to try to convince myself that there is no reason to feel this way, to push the feeling away, or to rush to solve it. The feeling is already here, and all I need to do is agree to meet it and be with it for a moment.

To breathe.

To give the feeling room.

And just as important, to understand myself.

Not to be alarmed that I am angry, worried or embarrassed, and not to see the very appearance of the feeling as proof that something is wrong with me.

It is normal to feel this way. This feeling makes sense.

I can say to myself:

“This really is worrying.”
“Of course this makes me angry.”
“It is hard for me to see my child like this.”

This is acceptance.

Not acceptance of the child's behavior, but acceptance of the feeling his behavior stirs up in me.

Accepting the feeling does not make the pain disappear. But when I try to run from the pain instead of acknowledging it, it grows stronger, turns into real suffering, and starts to run me. It is exactly when I stop, agree to meet the unpleasant feeling, give it a name, breathe and understand myself, that something softens. The pain is still there, but something in it becomes softer and easier to carry. That way it can pass through me instead of piling up.

And when we agree to meet this feeling, it no longer runs us, and something in the urgency weakens.

We no longer have to fix it right away. We do not have to silence it. We do not have to change the child in order to change what is happening inside us.

A space opens up inside us.

And in that space we can see the child and the situation more clearly.

Sometimes we will find that a boundary is needed. Sometimes we will find that it is right to respond differently. And sometimes we will find that there is nothing here that needs fixing.

Acceptance does not decide in advance what we will do.

It allows us to act out of choice, and not out of the urge to run from the feeling.

And that, to my mind, is where education begins.

Chapter 2: When His Pain Meets Me

We want so much for our children to be okay.

We invest in them, worry about them and try to protect them. Our dream is that they will be happy and safe, and that they will never have to meet pain, disappointment or insult.

But the dream that our child will always feel good is not realistic.

Here is a simple example: the child's popsicle fell on the floor.

He starts to cry, and in this moment his whole world has collapsed. I immediately want to do something. To buy him a new popsicle, to explain that it is not so terrible, or to distract him.

It is hard for me to simply stand there in front of his pain.

Because the child's pain also meets me, as a parent. And when it meets me, I rush to silence it with solutions, explanations, or attempts to convince the child that nothing terrible happened.

Usually, if I stop for a moment and really look, I will discover that more than I am trying to save the child from his pain, I am trying to save myself from the pain that his pain stirs up in me.

When I understand this, I can stop and come back to the child.

In this moment, the popsicle that fell is his whole world, and his disappointment is real. But it will not be the last disappointment he meets.

Over the course of his life there will be more things he wants, waits for and loves, and still they will get lost, break, or not work out the way he hoped. We will not always be able to fix it, and we will not always be next to him.

This moment is not just a glitch that needs to be solved quickly. It is a chance to be with him and help him discover that it is possible to meet disappointment, carry it and come through it.

So more than he needs a solution right now, he needs me to see his pain and be with him inside it.

To be able to do that, I first need to meet what his pain stirs up in me. To stop for a moment and acknowledge that it is hard for me to see him like this. That his pain hurts me, and that it is hard for me not to rush to fix what happened right away.

When I am able to meet my own pain as a parent, I am more available to meet the child's pain as well.

Instead of immediately buying another popsicle or explaining that it is only a popsicle, I can look at him and say:

“Wow, you wanted it so much, and now it fell. That is so disappointing.”

These are not words meant to make him stop crying. They let him understand what he is going through, feel that someone sees him, and discover that it is possible to be inside this feeling without being frightened by it. I am really trying to step into his shoes for a moment and meet the experience the way he feels it.

Very often, the simple fact that someone sees the child, understands him and is not frightened by his pain already calms something inside him. What happened still hurts, but the child is no longer alone inside the pain.

We meet this when the child fails a test, when a friend does not want to play with him, when he was not invited, or when something he waited for a long time does not happen the way he hoped.

Disappointment, insult, shame, jealousy, fear and loneliness are part of life. We cannot keep these feelings away from the child, and it is also not right to try to make them disappear every time they show up.

If every time we rush to solve the problem and put a band-aid on the feeling, the child will not learn to meet unpleasant feelings and carry them. Any pain may shake him, because he never got the chance to discover that even an unpleasant feeling is something you can feel and come through.

When we are able to stay next to him even when it hurts, he learns that it is possible to be disappointed without falling apart, to be hurt without running away, and to feel pain without being afraid of the pain itself.

We cannot promise the child a life without unpleasant feelings. We can be with him inside them today, and in this way help him build inside himself the ability to meet them, carry them, and little by little come through them on his own.

So what if he says no?

Chapter 3: Is My Authority Collapsing?

The child did not listen to me.

What is happening inside me right now?

Am I alarmed? Am I afraid my authority is collapsing? Do I feel I have to react immediately, so that the other siblings will not think it is possible not to listen to me? Am I acting out of fear of losing my place as a parent?

Sometimes the child's not listening touches a sensitive place inside me. For a moment I feel that if he does not listen to me, maybe I no longer have authority. And then a need rises in me to make him obey right away. Perhaps not only because my request is important or because a boundary is needed here, but also because I need his obedience in order to feel that I have not lost my place as a parent.

In that moment I need the child to do what I asked, so that I can calm down. And so, without noticing, his obedience becomes the confirmation I am looking for that I have not lost my place as a parent.

When I need the child to obey in order to restore my own confidence, my response grows out of the fear of losing authority, and not out of authority itself.

The steady ground

My confidence is not supposed to come from the child.

This confidence is something I need to build inside myself, out of my own inner work and out of the knowledge that I am the parent and the one who leads.

True, his not listening may shake me for a moment. But after I have made room for the fear that rose in me, I do not need to restore my confidence through immediate obedience. I can return to the steady ground inside me and know:

The child can argue, resist, and even not listen right now, and I do not lose my place as a parent because of it.

My place as a parent is not given to me when the child obeys, and it is not taken from me when he resists.

When I lean on this knowledge, I do not need his agreement or his immediate obedience in order to feel secure. I can stop, think, and choose how to respond well, without being dragged into a struggle to restore my confidence.

My authority is not decided all over again every moment according to what the child does, or according to how quickly he obeys me. It begins deep inside me.

When this confidence is in me, even when the child does not listen to me, the ground under my feet stays steady. It does not shake.

And when the ground under my feet is steady, it is easier for the child to feel safe as well. He feels that even when he resists, there is a steady parent in front of him who can keep leading him, not out of fear but out of authority.

Chapter 4: Not All Obedience Is Authority

“What kind of parent am I... even my own child does not listen to me.”

Many of us have this sentence go through our heads in moments of not being listened to. But it is exactly in these moments that the difference between obedience and authority is revealed.

Let us say the child did not listen, and I shouted, threatened or lost my temper, and in the end he did what I asked.

Did I win?

Maybe I got obedience in that moment, but the shouting and the threats did not grow out of authority. They grew out of pressure and the fear of losing control.

Obedience gained this way does not strengthen my authority. When shouting and threats become the way I get obedience, my authority actually wears away. The child learns to listen to the volume of my voice or to fear the threat, instead of listening because he knows I am the parent, that my word stands, and that he can rely on my leadership.

And slowly something else happens: the child learns that my word, when it is said in a calm voice, does not oblige him. As far as he is concerned, only the shouting or the threat signals that now he has to act. So my calm requests lose their power, and I have to raise my voice again and again to get the same listening.

I got obedience, but the way I got it weakened my word.

On the other hand, even if the child did not listen to me in that moment, and I still stayed calm, did not take back what I asked, and also did not lose my temper, I did not lose my authority. Quite the opposite.

The boundary does not disappear just because the child did not respond to it right away. Whatever still needs attention, a conversation or an educational response can be done after things calm down. There is no need to get obedience by shouting in order to prove that I am the parent.

It is natural for me to get angry when the child does not listen to me. My authority is tested in my ability to meet this anger, contain it, and not let it run my response and burst out at the child.

To stay calm, to stand by what I asked, and to contain the anger rising in me without taking it out on the child: to my mind, that is real authority.

The child also feels it when I stand in front of him calm and secure. He understands that his not listening does not shake me and that I do not need to shout in order to lead him. Even if he still does not listen in that moment, over time it is exactly this steadiness that strengthens my authority in his eyes.

A value, or a need of mine?

Chapter 5: Wait… Why, Actually?

One morning, in the middle of tefillah in class, I saw a few students playing with their hands together.

Quietly. Without disturbing anyone.

I was already getting ready to say something to them, and then I stopped for a moment and asked myself:

Wait… why, actually?

Is there really something wrong here that calls for a boundary?

Or is it simply hard for me to see movement, a lack of order, children who are not sitting exactly the way I imagined?

Am I acting out of a value, or out of a need for control, a fear of losing authority, and a wish for everything to look disciplined and orderly?

After all, they were not disturbing the tefillah and they were not drawing the attention of the other students. It was simply hard for me to see them sitting differently from what I expected. The remark that was about to come out of me did not come from an educational value, but from my own need for everything to look orderly. When I understood that, I stopped.

That moment reminded me that not everything I do not like calls for a boundary or for educational action.

It is a small example, but in everyday life there are many like it.

So every time I come to educate, I first need to check myself:

Where am I acting from?

Am I acting from a personal, pressured, triggered place?

Is this a behavior that only bothers me personally?

Or is there a real value here that I want to educate toward?

And if so, what is that value?

To my mind, the line between these two places is thin. Very thin.

This inquiry is one of our life's tasks as parents and educators: to check again and again where the boundaries we set are coming from.

Sometimes we will find that there is a real value here, and for its sake a boundary is truly needed.

And sometimes we will find that the boundary is mainly meant to fill a personal need of ours.

There may also be cases where we say the very same words and set exactly the same boundary, but from completely different places.

In one case the boundary will come from a personal, pressured and frightened place.

And in another case the same boundary will come from a place of values, clean and constructive.

The same words.

The same boundary.

And a world of difference between them.

To my mind, this is a life's work.

Chapter 6: The Subtlest Lie in Education

We were at a restaurant.

The children were wonderful, but part of the time, the way children are, they got a little wild.

It embarrassed me.

As we left, I was already thinking:

“Before next time, I will talk to them about how to behave in a restaurant.”

And then I asked myself:

How should they really behave?

Quietly?

Respectfully?

And why, actually?

The answer that came up in me right away was:

“Because it bothers other people.”

But right away I said to myself:

“Liar. That is not really what bothers you.

What bothers you is that you are embarrassed.”

And that was the truth.

Without that embarrassment, I am not sure the matter would have been so important to me. I did not really think the children were creating a disturbance the people around them could not handle. All they did was get a little playful, the way children do.

Only after the embarrassment rose in me did I try to dress it up in nice words like “consideration” and “values,” in order to turn my personal need into an act of education.

That does not mean there is no value in being considerate of others. Of course there is. There are also cases where children do disturb others, and then a boundary is certainly needed.

But in this case, when I checked myself honestly, I understood that it was not the value that was moving me.

It was my embarrassment.

And as I already wrote, this is a thin line. Very thin.

In another case, the very same behavior might call for a boundary, because it really does hurt others or goes against a value I want to educate toward.

But in this case I felt that what was moving me was not the educational value, but the personal, triggered place inside me.

And when that is the place I am acting from, I am in no position to educate.

I can, of course, say to the children:

“Kids, this embarrasses me. As far as I am concerned, we only go to a restaurant when we know how to behave calmly.”

At least that would be the truth.

As a parent, I am allowed to set rules and conditions that come from my own personal needs too, as long as I am honest with myself and do not present them as something they are not.

But I do not want to take a personal need, dress it up in words like “consideration” and “values,” and then call it education.

I do not want to be a lying educator.

An educator who dresses up his own wishes in nice words.

It is so easy. It is also comfortable.

But it is a lie, and there is certainly no education in it.

As much as I can, I want my education to grow out of values and not out of personal motives that I do not notice at first glance.

This inquiry does not end with the question of whether or not to set a boundary.

Sometimes we will set exactly the same boundary and say exactly the same words, and still act from completely different places.

In one case it will come from a personal, pressured, triggered place.

And in another case it will grow from a place of values, clean and constructive.

The same words.

The same boundary.

And a world of difference between them.

I did my part. What about the rest?

Chapter 7: The Birthday Party

I went to a lot of trouble, ran around and worked very hard on the birthday party I put together for my daughter at home.

It was an elaborate party with all the girls in her class.

Shopping, preparations, arrangements, cleaning, organizing...

And in the end?

You guessed it.

Complaints. And more complaints. And more complaints.

Not complaints about me, and not grievances about the party I made for her.

There were simply things that did not work out the way she wanted. In one of the games it was too crowded for her, and other things that happened during the party frustrated and annoyed her.

And still, the sentence almost rose up in me:

“What are you complaining about? Look how much I put into this for you!”

As if the fact that I worked hard for her obliges her to be happy and satisfied.

But I stopped myself.

I stopped for a moment and tried to meet what had risen in me. The disappointment, the frustration, and the helplessness in the face of all the effort I had put in.

Only after that could I really make room, try to step into her shoes, and say:

“You waited so long for this birthday and imagined how it would be. And now not everything is working out the way you wanted. Ugh, that is disappointing...”

When I said these words, I was not trying to describe her disappointment from the outside. I was trying to really experience the feeling together with her for a moment and give it words.

Because there is something important to understand:

As a parent, I do not own what my daughter feels.

What we invest in our children is not a contract that obliges them to feel what we hoped they would feel.

The fact that I worked hard and invested does not oblige her to feel happiness specifically. She is allowed to be frustrated, disappointed or tired too.

When I expect my investment to buy me her happiness, I turn what I gave her as a gift into a debt she has to repay. And she feels it. Instead of receiving a gift, she receives a demand.

And what about gratitude?

True, I want her to see how much people went to trouble and invested for her, and not to take it for granted.

But gratitude does not oblige her to be happy and satisfied every moment. She can appreciate what we did for her, and at the same time be disappointed by things that did not work out the way she wanted.

And even if I want to educate her toward gratitude, I need to ask myself whether this is the right time to do it, in the middle of the frustration, the pressure and the mess, or whether it is better to talk about it later, calmly.

So at this stage the right thing is simply to meet her feeling, be with her inside it, and reflect in words what she is feeling.

After she felt that I was with her, I told her it was worth trying to be flexible and not get stuck. But I also made peace with the possibility that this is what she was feeling right now, and that maybe this is how the party would end.

In the end, by the way, she enjoyed it very much.

But it does not always end that way.

We can plan a birthday for the child down to the last detail, invest in a family trip or prepare a festive meal. We do all of this with love and out of a wish for him to be happy, but we cannot manage what will rise in his heart. He may be disappointed, feel frustrated, or simply not be in the mood to enjoy it.

Even when his response disappoints us, and even when we wanted so much for him to be happy and satisfied, we need to remember that the child's inner world remains his own, and he does not have to feel differently in order to fit our expectations.

Chapter 8: Not Becoming One More Child in the Fight

Before the vacation, I prepare myself with one sentence that I say to myself:

During the vacation there will be fights between the siblings.

Not maybe.

There will be.

I even set myself a realistic target: let us say, fifty fights.

Not because fights are a good thing or because I am aiming for that target, but because they are a natural part of siblings living together.

When I take this into account in advance, I spare myself at least part of the pressure and alarm that fights between siblings stir up in us as parents.

Because for me, the main question is not only how the children will behave during the fight. The question is what we, as parents, will do.

Will we jump right into it?

Start investigating who started it?

Raise our voice?

Get sucked into the unpleasant atmosphere, and in the end become one more child in the fight ourselves?

Sometimes, in spite of preparing in advance, I still feel that I have no energy left and that their fight is creating pressure inside me. In moments like these, I simply step away a little.

If I have a quiet corner in the house, I go to it.

And sometimes I even step out for a short while to somewhere nearby to get some air.

I do not do this when the fight puts one of the children in danger. In a situation like that, I stay and step in.

But when I decide that it is possible to let them deal with it themselves, I tell them:

“Whoever wants can come with me, calmly. I am going out for a few minutes to calm down.”

I sit for a bit, breathe and calm down.

And when I come back, I come back more settled and with renewed strength.

And in the meantime, in many cases, they have also had time to calm down, talk to each other, and even make up.

This may sound a little cruel.

To leave the children with their fight and simply walk away?

But for two reasons, it is actually the opposite.

First, when I step out of the fight and leave them space to deal with it, I am telling them that I trust them.

We talk a lot at home about the tools that help with a fight. We have learned how to stop, how to talk and how to solve.

Now I am giving them a chance to use these tools on their own.

I can say:

“I am going out for a few minutes, and I trust you to try to solve the fight by the time I get back.”

And second, my presence does not always help.

Sometimes the very fact that I stay there, irritated and worn out, only feeds the fire. In trying to get them to stop right away, I get sucked into the tension, raise my voice, and add my own anger to the fight.

And more than that: when the children see that in a moment of low energy I do not get sucked in, but stop, step away a little and come back calmer, they learn something important about handling the fight itself as well.

They learn that it is possible to stop for a moment.

To breathe.

To step away when needed.

And then to come back.

And when I come back to them, I come back as their father.

Not as one more child in the fight.

Not every fight requires us to solve it right away.

Sometimes the most parental thing we can do is to stop.
Step away.
And come back with strength.

What do I get out of this?

Chapter 9: The Hidden Gain

Sometimes a behavior does not stay with us only because it is hard to change, but also because it gives us something.

In psychology this is called “secondary gain”: something we get out of a situation, even when the situation itself is not pleasant for us.

For example:

A child who is hurt again and again by his brother and rushes every time to come and tell us.

Clearly he wants us to help him. But beyond that, there may also be another gain here: sometimes turning to us saves him from having to cope on his own, and sometimes through it he gets something he needs very much: attention, comfort, a hug, a feeling that he is seen, cared for and protected.

That does not mean he is doing it on purpose. But if this way of handling the situation fills his need, it may grow stronger and stay, even without his being aware of it.

The hidden gain can also be found in the parent, who gets from the pattern a feeling of being needed, or satisfaction from being the one who calms, solves and holds the home together.

Here too, the point is not that the parent does this consciously. But this need can affect the way he responds, and cause him, without noticing, to keep strengthening the same pattern again and again.

And this is an important point:

Even when no one wants the situation, and even when everyone suffers from it, there may still be a “certain gain” that helps it stay.

So when we meet a pattern that keeps repeating and hurts us, it is worth asking not only: “Why is this happening?”

But also: “What am I getting out of this situation?”

And the deeper question is: am I really willing to give up what I get from it, so that it can truly change?

Chapter 10: What Does the Hidden Gain Look Like in Real Life?

As we said, even inside a situation that is hard for us, there may be something hidden that we get out of it. Here are a few examples of what it looks like in real life:

The child who regularly sleeps in the parents' bed

We say:

“I wake up tired, my back hurts, I have no privacy. It cannot go on like this.”

But despite what we say, we are not always really trying to stop it. Sometimes we simply let it go on.

Maybe because alongside the difficulty, there is also something there that feels good to us.

The closeness. The hug. The feeling that he needs me.

And maybe it is simply much easier to let him into the bed than to deal right now with crying, resistance and a struggle.

In other words, we really do suffer from the situation, but there is also something in it that keeps us inside it.

The spouse who does everything at home alone

“I am collapsing. No one helps me. Everything always falls on me.”

That may really be the situation.

But maybe, alongside the difficulty, there is also something here that I get: I do not have to ask for help. I do not have to explain how I want things done. I do not risk being disappointed if the other side does not do it exactly the way I hoped.

And in the meantime I also stay the one who knows, who arranges, who holds everything together.

In other words, I really do suffer from the load, but there may also be something inside this situation that is hard for me to give up.

The parent who gives in too quickly

“They just do not listen to me, I do not even try anymore.”

So I give a few more minutes of screen time, one more candy, and give in one more time.

And it brings quiet.

The child is satisfied, I no longer have to face his frustration, and in that moment it is easier.

In other words, I really do suffer from having no boundaries, but giving in spares me the hard moment of standing in front of his crying and his anger.

So yes, afterward I complain that there are no boundaries.

But in the moment itself, giving in gave me something:

Quiet.

For the sake of that quiet, I give in again the next time too.

And that is the point.

Sometimes the thing we want so much to change also gives us something.

And sometimes it is exactly this thing that is part of what keeps us in the same place.

So when there is a situation that comes back again and again, and we say:

“Enough, I have to change this,”

it is worth stopping to ask:

What am I getting out of this situation?

What does it give me?

And what will I have to give up, if I really want it to change?

Part II: The Feeling Behind the Behavior

What helps me choose well?

Chapter 11: Feelings Drive Behavior

People tend to think the problem is that we are too focused on the present.

If a tempting food is in front of us, even when we know that later we will pay a price for it, we may give in:

“I know I will regret it later, but right now it is so tempting...”

There is truth in this, but it is not the main point.

What usually moves us is not only what we think, but mainly what we feel.

You can think of feeling as the engine of behavior. Reason sits next to it, and sometimes even puts a hand on the wheel, but feeling is what pushes us forward.

So when we try to overcome an urge or change a habit, it is not enough to talk only to reason.

We can explain to ourselves again and again the price, the benefit and all the logical considerations. But in the moment of temptation, these explanations are not always strong enough.

To influence a feeling, you have to speak its language.

To imagine the sense of satisfaction and worth we will feel after we overcome it.

To imagine the pleasure of the compliments we will get for the effort and for the change.

To imagine how we will feel in our body, our energy and our health over time.

The more tangible the future positive feeling is, and the more we really feel it, the greater the chance that we will manage to choose well in the present.

To deal with one feeling, you need to call up another feeling.

When we connect the behavior we want to feelings of satisfaction, worth and meaning, an engine forms inside us that can help us choose well even in moments of temptation.

And from here, to education...

Chapter 12: When the Child Learns to Feel the Good

If feelings drive behavior, this is true not only of adults, but perhaps even more of children.

We often educate through logic. We explain to the child what is right and what is not right, what is allowed and what is not, and how one should behave.

These explanations are important, but they are not always what drives his behavior.

If we want good behavior to become part of the child's character, it is not enough for him to know that it is right. It is worth helping him also notice the feeling that comes with it.

When the child is considerate, helps, overcomes something or acts responsibly, we can draw his attention to what the act stirred up in him:

“It looks like you feel good about what you did.”

“How do you feel now, after you managed to overcome it?”

It is important that this be a real question. We are not trying to put into the child's mouth the feeling we expect him to feel, but inviting him to listen to what is really happening inside him. He may feel satisfaction, and he may not. We accept his answer as it is. Otherwise, instead of connecting him to himself, we have only taught him which answer we want to hear.

When the child learns to stop and notice what is really happening inside him, he begins to recognize the connection between his choices and the feelings that stay with him afterward.

It is important to remember that good behavior is not always easy or pleasant in the moment itself.

Sometimes it asks the child to hold back, give something up, make an effort or overcome an urge. But after the act, it is usually accompanied by a deeper and more pleasant feeling: satisfaction, a sense of worth, closeness, or a feeling that I acted rightly.

The same is true in the other direction.

Inappropriate behavior may give the child a feeling of power, release or momentary relief. But afterward it usually leaves him with a less pleasant feeling.

In moments like these too, we can help him stop and look:

“What feeling stayed with you after what you did?”

“Is that a feeling you like staying with?”

The goal is not to make the child feel guilty, but to help him recognize a simple connection that exists in life:

Our choices affect not only what happens around us, but also what we feel inside ourselves afterward.

The more aware the child becomes of the feelings his actions leave in him, the more an inner compass will develop inside him.

A compass that will help him choose well not only because an adult is next to him, explaining or warning him, but because he himself knows the feeling that comes with choosing the good.

Because in the end, it is not enough to teach a child what is right to do.

It is also important to help him feel the good, and little by little, to love it.

Am I waiting for him, or expecting from him?

Chapter 13: When the Child Falls Apart at Home

The child came home from gan or school, and he is falling apart?

He cries over everything.
He gets annoyed easily.
He argues.
He has no patience.

We immediately try to understand what happened to him. Who hurt him? What went wrong today? And sometimes we just want this behavior to stop, and we say:

“Enough crying over everything.”

“Why are you behaving like this?”

But it may be that nothing unusual happened. The child simply held himself together for hours, and now he has no strength left to keep going.

Spending long hours in gan or school asks a great deal of the child: to concentrate, to hold back, to overcome, to adjust to rules and to wait for what he wants.

When he gets home, to the place where he feels safe, he finally allows himself to let go of everything he held inside during the day, and it comes out in crying, irritability and outbursts.

So what is the right thing to do?

First of all, to understand that this is natural and not to be alarmed.

We do not have to rush to investigate, explain, educate or solve. Sometimes the child mainly needs time, quiet and our presence, until his body and his feelings calm down.

We can also reflect back to him what is going on inside him and put it into words, so that little by little he learns to understand himself:

“You must be really tired and worn out from your day at school. I understand you.”

Sometimes our understanding itself, together with a little time, quiet and a calm presence, is exactly what the child needs to reset and come back to himself.

Of course, our understanding of the difficulty does not cancel the boundaries. If falling apart turns into hurtful behavior, we will stop it, but we will do it out of understanding for what he is going through, calmly, and not out of anger or alarm.

And maybe it is worth remembering: if he falls apart specifically at home, it may be a good sign. Here he feels safe enough to stop holding himself together.

Chapter 14: Give Him a Moment to Get Home

The child comes back from gan or school. He has only just walked in, and we are already showering him with questions:

“How was today?”
“What did you learn?”
“Who did you play with?”
“Did anything happen?”
“Come on, tell me!”

We are excited to see him and try to stir the same excitement in him, but he answers weakly:

“Fine.”

But maybe this is simply not what he needs right now.

After a day full of experiences, challenges, wants, encounters and effort, he may need a little quiet. A calm place where he can put down his bag, breathe and arrive home, not only in his body but also in his feelings.

And this is true not only of questions. Sometimes we are waiting for a hug. We missed him all day, and now we want to feel him close to us.

But sometimes the hug we want so much does not suit the child in that moment. He is tired, overwhelmed, or simply not open to touch. And still we hug a little longer, even when his body is already signaling to us that it does not feel good to him.

In moments like these it is worth stopping to ask ourselves:

Who is this hug for, me or the child?

And who are all the questions for, my curiosity and my need to know, or because he really wants to tell me now?

There is nothing wrong with questions or with a hug. Some children will want to tell about their day right away and will be happy to run into our arms.

But instead of deciding in advance what the child needs, it is worth meeting him and listening to what he is signaling to us.

Sometimes he will want to talk and hug.

And sometimes he will only need us to welcome him happily and give him the space he needs.

The fact that he is our child does not mean that his body, his time and his closeness are at our disposal at every moment.

Even within the closest relationship, our role is to listen to what suits him and adjust ourselves to his pace, and not to demand that he fill our need for closeness when it does not suit him.

Is it allowed to be unpleasant?

Chapter 15: Not Being Afraid of Feelings

Sometimes the child manages to throw us off balance.

He behaves in a way that shakes our calm and even makes us angry. The anger starts to build up, and we feel that in another moment it will come out on the child.

And it is not always only because of the child.

Maybe we came home tired, worried or frustrated about something else. But our patience is already short, and any little thing may make us unload what we are feeling onto the children of all people.

This is exactly the place to practice accepting the feeling, as we saw in the first chapter.

When an unpleasant feeling shows up, trying to push it away or to act as if it is not there usually does not make it disappear. It keeps building up inside us, until it finds a way out.

Instead, we can stop for a moment.

Breathe.

Notice what is happening inside us and call the feeling by its name:

I am angry.
I am worried.
I am sad.
I am overwhelmed.

We can even say to the children:

“Abba is angry right now. I need a few minutes to calm down, and then I will come back to you.”

Or:

“Abba is a little worried and sad right now. It is not because of you. I need a few minutes by myself, and then I will come back.”

The goal is not to make the children partners in everything we are going through, and certainly not to make them feel that they need to calm us down.

The goal is to show them that Abba and Ima have feelings too, and that it is possible to meet them without being alarmed and without unloading them onto others.

Some parents are afraid to let their children see them having unpleasant feelings. They fear it will make them look weak in their children's eyes.

I think exactly the opposite.

A father who can say to his children, “I am angry right now, I need a few minutes,” is a strong father. Even when he is angry, worried or sad, he is able to recognize the feeling, call it by its name, and not let it run him and hurt others.

And what if I did not manage to stop?

I know how all of this may sound.

“Oh, come on. Every time I get angry I will stop, breathe, name the feeling and talk to it? Who can live like that?”

It may sound like one of those nice pieces of advice you read in a book, agree with, and then cannot apply when it really counts.

But the goal is not to succeed at this a hundred percent. At first we will not always manage to stop in time, and that too is part of the way.

Sometimes we will recognize the feeling only after we have already reacted, shouted, or said something we did not want to say.

But even then we can practice.

We can stop after the fact and ask ourselves:

What did I feel in that moment? What set me off? What happened inside me before I burst out?

The goal is not to succeed right away, but to learn to know ourselves.

The more we practice looking at the feeling even after the fact, the closer we will begin to notice it to the moment it appeared.

Later we may notice it in the middle of reacting, and with time, even before it takes over.

Little by little, we learn to be more precise.

I know this process from myself too. This practice has been with me for years, and even today I do not always manage to stop in time. But over the years I have learned to recognize more quickly what is happening inside me. When I recognize the feeling and give it room, it runs me less.

And when I stay for a moment with myself and with the feeling, I can say inside:

“It is natural that I feel this way.”

When we allow the feeling to be, call it by its name, breathe and show understanding toward ourselves, the pain does not always disappear right away, but the struggle against it lessens.

The feeling becomes more bearable, and inside the difficulty a pleasant sense of understanding appears too:

I see myself. I am not frightened of myself. I can get through this too.

This way we do not become weaker in the children's eyes. Quite the opposite.

Through our personal example, they learn that there is no need to be afraid of unpleasant feelings.

They see that it is possible to feel anger, worry or sadness, give them room, and know that they will change and pass.

And so, little by little, both we and the children learn to meet the next unpleasant feeling without running from it and without taking it out on the people around us.

Chapter 16: Ima, I'm Bored

This sentence can immediately make our heart tighten a little.

The child does not feel good. He is restless. He does not know what to do with himself.

And within a moment, we start looking for solutions:

“Here, take a game.”
“Why don't you draw?”
“Maybe invite a friend?”
“You have so many things to do.”

But sometimes it is worth stopping to ask:

Who is having a harder time right now, the child or us?

The child is meeting a moment of boredom, but his feeling grows stronger inside us.

It is hard for us to see him dissatisfied, frustrated or helpless. So we rush to get rid of the feeling.

Not necessarily because he needs a solution right away, but because we find it hard to stay next to him when he is not happy.

When the child says to me, “I'm bored,” I can make room for what he is feeling:

“Wow… I understand you.”

It does not always take a whole sentence. Sometimes one word is enough, or an understanding look.

I can be with him inside the feeling for a moment, without rushing to find a solution for him.

After we have been with him in the feeling, and we are no longer acting out of pressure to make it disappear, we can help the child take responsibility for the solution himself.

We can ask:

“What do you say, what could we do?”

Sometimes he will have an idea.

Sometimes he will need a little help to get started.

And sometimes he will simply stay a while longer inside the boredom.

And that is okay.

Boredom can have value too. It is exactly out of the moments when nothing is keeping the child busy that an idea, a game or creativity may grow, things that would not have appeared if we had rushed to fill every free moment for him.

But that is not the most important point.

What matters is that the child learns that not every discomfort is a danger, and not every unpleasant feeling has to disappear right away.

We stay next to him and show him our full trust: he is able to meet the unpleasant feeling, and later also to think, come up with ideas and find a solution on his own.

Chapter 17: I Understand You - and Still, No

Sometimes, when we set a boundary for the child, it seems to us that we have to choose:

Either be with him, or tell him no.

Usually we are pulled toward one of the two extremes. Either we say “no” quickly and harshly, so that there is no room for argument. Or we feel so strongly how much he wants it that we cannot stand it, and we give in.

In both cases we are really running from the same moment: the moment when we have to stand in front of the child's strong wish and his disappointment. We run from it either into a rigid, closed “no,” or into giving in.

But the truth is that not only is there no contradiction here, sometimes it is exactly the connection to the feeling that allows the boundary to be steadier and gentler.

The girl asks for another cookie. We can say:

“Wow, you really want it. It is so tasty, and you really feel like having another one.”

The boy wants to keep going on the computer. We can say:

“Wow, you feel like going on so much. I know, it's tempting. It is really hard to stop now.”

Not to say it just with our lips. To really stop. To really see him. To really feel, for a moment, how much he wants it.

And then to say:

“And still, no.”

Not out of anger. Not out of disconnection. Not because his feeling does not matter.

I can give his feeling all the room in the world. But I do not change the boundary just so that he will not have to meet the disappointment.

When I make room for his feelings and still say “no,” the boundary no longer stands between us like a wall. I am not on one side with the child on the other. I am next to him, facing the difficulty.

What I am really telling him is:

“I see how much you want it. I understand that it is hard. I am staying with you. And the answer is still no.”

The words themselves matter less. What matters is that the child feels I am not fighting his feeling. I am not trying to convince him that he does not really want it, that it is not so terrible, or that he is exaggerating. I understand how much he wants it, and I agree that it may be hard.

A child can be angry at the boundary, be disappointed by it and even cry, and still feel that his parent is on his side.

The boundary stays, but it no longer separates us. I am really with his strong wish, really identifying with it. And instead of distance and struggle, connection forms.

Acknowledging the feeling is not giving up the boundary. Sometimes it is exactly what makes it steadier, closer and safer.

Part III: Instead of Punishment

Am I educating, or paying him back?

Chapter 18: I Don't Usually Punish

I do not usually punish, and my home is not run in an atmosphere of punishments. I am not looking every time for what to take away from the child, what to keep from him, or what price to make him pay so that he will learn his lesson.

Not because I think we need to feel sorry for the child, and not because I do not believe in authority and in setting boundaries.

Quite the opposite.

Boundaries are among the healthiest and most necessary things for a child, and really for an adult too.

But a boundary is not a punishment, and authority is not built through fear. To my mind, most punishments actually come from a lack of authority.

When a parent cannot get a boundary kept through presence, consistency and firmness, he tries to back up his words with a price:

“If you don't listen, you'll lose it.”
“If you do that again, you won't get it.”

Instead of the child listening because Abba or Ima set a boundary, he is supposed to listen because he is afraid of the punishment.

But that is not authority. It is an attempt to make up for its absence.

In other cases the punishment comes out of anger, frustration, hurt or helplessness.

The child does not listen, the parent feels he is losing control, and in that moment a need rises in him to make the child pay a price.

Sometimes there is even something of revenge in it:

“You made me feel helpless, and now I will make you feel the price.”

We do not always say this to ourselves, and we certainly do not always do it consciously. But if we stop and honestly check the feeling the punishment was born from, we will find that more than once it was meant to calm our own helplessness more than to educate the child.

When I am an authority, in most cases I do not need to get there.

I set a clear boundary. I stay calm. I repeat the request without being alarmed. And I stand behind my words.

The child does not have to like the boundary, but he learns that I mean what I say.

He sees that I am not alarmed by his resistance, by his anger, or by the fact that he does not listen right away. I do not lose my calm, I do not back away from the boundary, and I also do not feel a need to threaten him with punishment so that he will take me seriously.

I stay calm, clear and consistent, and that is how authority is built.

So the question is not only whether punishments work. The deeper question is why we need them in the first place.

But even if we judge punishment only by the result, it is not at all clear that it achieves its goal.

On the face of it, it sounds very logical:

If the child knows he is going to pay a price for his actions, he will be wary of repeating them. But is that really what we see?

In homes where there is a lot of punishment, do children stop breaking the rules? Does everything become quiet and calm? Not necessarily.

Punishment may stop the child in that moment, and may teach him to be careful not to get caught next time. But it will not necessarily help him understand what he did, take responsibility and learn to act differently.

That is why my main path is not punishment, but authority and a process.

When the boundary is not kept

And still, the child does not always listen. Sometimes he breaks a rule, or goes against a value that is important to me. So what do I do?

First of all, I check myself.

Where am I acting from right now?

Am I acting out of educational responsibility and out of the importance of the boundary, or because I am hurt, alarmed or angry?

If I am upset, it is better for me to stop first. Otherwise the boundary may become a way to unload onto the child what I am feeling.

But if it is clear to me that I am acting out of educational responsibility, I can use my authority.

I say, in a calm but firm tone:

“Akiva, I asked you not to do that.”

If he keeps going, I repeat it in the same tone.

There is no need to shout. There is no need to threaten. There is no need to launch into a speech.

Sometimes there is no need to say anything. A clear gesture of the hand, or a look, is enough.

Akiva keeps going?

I wait a little and remind him again.

I am not alarmed that the boundary was not accepted right away, and I do not try to shorten the process with a shout or a threat. I have the patience to stay in my place and require that the boundary be kept.

I have no patience?

That is already my own work.

But what happens when the child keeps going again and again and nothing changes?

For cases like these, I have learned two meaningful tools.

Before I present them, I want to make one thing clear:

These tools too must never be used out of hurt, anger or a wish to get back at the child. If I use them to pay him back for not listening to me, I have lost their educational purpose.

The first tool: the choice

I say:

“Akiva, I asked you to stop playing now. If you choose to listen and stop, you are choosing to keep the game. If you choose to keep going and not listen, you are choosing for the game to be taken away.”

I repeat it one more time, so that there is no doubt:

“Stop and keep the game, or keep going and lose it. The choice is yours.”

After that, I count to three.

If Akiva keeps playing, the choice has been made and the game is taken away.

I do not argue. I do not negotiate. And I do not take it back because of the crying, the pressure or the promises that come after the choice.

I also do not get angry, and I do not say:

“I told you.”

I stay calm.

The child knew in advance what was going to happen, was given two clear options, and chose between them.

The power of this tool is not in the threat or in counting to three, but in the certainty.

The difference from punishment is that the result is known in advance, and the child is the one who chooses it. When he chooses to keep playing, he is also choosing for the game to be taken away.

Of course, before I offer the child a choice like this, I need to make sure the boundary is clear and that the result is proportionate and fair.

I use this tool only rarely, and only when there is a real need for it. If we turn every request into a choice between obeying and losing, we will wear the tool out and turn the relationships at home into a string of threats and deals.

But when it is used in moderation, from a calm place and out of authority, it is a very powerful tool.

The second tool: a personal talk

We do not have the talk in the middle of the incident itself.

In that moment we only say:

“Akiva, what happened here needs a personal talk with Abba and Ima. We will wait for Ima to come home, and then we will sit down and talk about it.”

Until the talk takes place, the extras stop.

No friends, no candy and no special requests. We do not carry on with the routine as if nothing happened, before we have stopped to deal seriously with what took place.

You might ask: isn't this just punishment in a different form?

In my opinion, no. A punishment is a price placed on the child so that he will feel what he did. Here there is no price. There is a pause. If candy happens to be handed out, we save his for after the talk. He does not lose anything. But until the talk takes place, life does not go on as usual.

True, it is not pleasant for him. But meeting a boundary and responsibility is not always pleasant, and that is okay. There is no need to rush to rescue him from every discomfort.

Even if after a while the child asks to have the talk already, we will not necessarily do it right away. The talk will take place at a time we decide is right, when we are truly available for it. If Abba is tired or not available right now, he can say: “I know you want us to talk now, but this is not the right time. We will talk after supper.”

We will not leave the child in uncertainty. We will give him as clear a time as possible and have the talk when we said we would. When the time comes, we sit in a quiet place, in an atmosphere that makes clear we are taking what happened seriously.

We do not go back to arguing about the past. We saw what happened, and the act itself is clear to us. So we do not get into who started it, or whether the child meant it or not.

What happened has already happened. Now the question is how to make sure it does not happen again.

So we say:

“Akiva, what happened before cannot happen again. I want you to think and tell me what you plan to do so that it does not happen again.”

And then I am quiet.

I do not lecture, explain, or offer him answers. I simply sit with him quietly and let him think. Now it is his time.

If the child starts to explain:

“But he started it...”
“I didn't mean to...”
“It only happened because...”

we do not get into an argument and we do not try to refute every claim. We bring him back to the question:

“We are not talking now about who started it. I want to know what you plan to do so that it does not happen again.”

Some children find this silence hard. They will try to explain, to blame, or to get away from the question. We do not argue with them. We bring them back to the question and again let them think quietly. Other children will offer an idea quickly.

Then too we can ask: “And what else could help you?”

The extra question invites him to go a little deeper, and not to settle for the first answer just to end the talk.

We do not rush to end the talk. We give it about fifteen minutes, and we end it when we feel that the child has taken responsibility and thought seriously about how he will act differently.

At the end we tell him: “I believe you that you mean it seriously. I am giving you another chance, and I expect you to try with all your strength so that it does not happen again.”

This talk is not necessarily pleasant for the child. And that is okay.

In the silence and the seriousness, the child meets himself and understands that it is not enough to say “sorry” and run on. He needs to think about how he plans to change his behavior. There is room for regret and remorse over what happened, but the main purpose of the talk is to help him think about how he will repair things and act differently from now on.

And if it happens again? We have another talk. We do not say right away: “You see? I can't believe you.”

I believe that last time he really did mean what he said. But the way he thought of was not enough, so he needs to stop and think again. After that I will give him another chance and believe him again that he plans to try with all his strength. And so, from talk to talk, a process takes place. In most cases I see the incidents become fewer.

Not because he is afraid of punishment. The talk is not pleasant for him, but it is not meant to punish him. It is meant to stop him and make him think. He knows we will not let things slide, and that each time he will have to stop, face his responsibility, and figure out how he will act differently.

Only in exceptional cases, when I see that the talks have lost their meaning and the child no longer takes them seriously, will I consider imposing a sanction:

“For the coming week, no going to friends.” This I call a punishment.

Even then it will not be given out of revenge or an outburst, but out of a considered decision that the usual tools are not enough right now. But it is an exceptional tool.

It is not the atmosphere the home runs on, and it is not the first way I educate. The main path is to set a clear boundary, stand by it calmly and consistently, and let the child meet his responsibility time after time, until a real change forms inside him.

Chapter 19: One Thing, Two at Most

Some parents will read the previous chapter and say:

“Wait, a quarter of an hour for each talk, times ten, times eight children. Who can keep that up?”

So I want to make clear a basic principle of real educational work.

Education cannot be run only from the gut, with responses we pull out of our sleeve every time something bothers us.

At any given period, we do not work with the child on dozens of things at once.

We choose one thing, two at most, that are the red lines we are focusing on now.

We can also share this with the children or the students:

“For the coming period we are focusing on these two things. This is what we are working on now, and this is where we expect to see a change.”

That way it is clear to everyone what is in focus right now.

That does not mean we ignore every other behavior or give up the other boundaries at home. When a behavior needs to be stopped, we stop it.

But not everything needs a whole process, a personal talk and ongoing follow-up right now.

We put most of our strength into the one or two points we chose to move forward.

This is education with focus.

Not education by overload.

When the goal is clear, expectations are aligned. The parent knows what he is standing firm on, and the child knows what is expected of him. That way it is possible to be consistent and to keep the process going over time.

On the other hand, when we react from the gut and scatter ourselves over dozens of points, we come out exhausted, the children no longer understand what really matters, and we have no way of being consistent.

When we try to fix everything at once, our strength gets scattered and it is hard to keep up a change over time.

It is better to choose the thing that matters now, put our strength into it, and stay with it until a change starts to happen.

Part IV: Raising Independence and Confidence

Does he really need me to do it for him?

Chapter 20: Not Doing It for Him

Sometimes I see my child working hard to do something, and it is already clear to me how the task could be finished faster.

In everyday life there are countless moments like these. It might be a small child trying to put on his socks, a girl making herself something to eat, or a child trying to put a game together and not managing.

I see him working hard. It takes time, his way is sometimes clumsy, and almost without noticing I say:

“Come, let me. I'll do it for you.”

I really do mean to help. But in these situations, the help the child needs from me is that I wait a little, encourage him, and believe that he can keep trying on his own.

If I rush to finish the task for him, maybe I will save time and mess, but he will not get the chance to try all the way to the end and discover that he can.

When the first way does not work, he looks for another way. He gets it wrong, corrects it and tries again.

For that to happen, I try to stop and not rush to take the task away from him.

Instead of doing it for him, I can be with him inside the attempt and give words to what is happening:

“Ugh, that almost worked. There, you're trying again.”
“You're not giving up.”

He will probably do it more slowly, make mistakes, and maybe even leave a little mess along the way. That is part of learning.

And when he finally succeeds, he will be able to look at what he did and know:

“I did that.”

It may only be a sock he managed to put on by himself, something he made to eat, or a game he put together. But for the child, it is one more experience in which he discovered that he can.

These small experiences add up, and out of them, little by little, his confidence in himself is built.

Letting Him Try

Not doing it for him does not only mean not taking the task away from him. Sometimes we are afraid our child will fail, and before he has even tried, we are already stopping him.

I remember the time my son, who was about three then or maybe even younger, wanted to climb the slide from the bottom up. I was about to tell him not to try, because it looked too hard to me and I thought failing would only disappoint him. And while I was thinking about how to stop him, I saw that he was already at the top. That moment caught me: sometimes we have no idea how far a child can go, and we may stop him only because we are afraid of the disappointment he might feel.

I see the same thing in the classroom. I make a habit of giving my students a lot of responsibility and trusting them: with personal jobs, with class committees, and especially by making room for their ideas and their initiatives. Slowly it sinks in:

Two third-grade students organized a class bonfire on their own. Another group of students organized a class evening. And students who decided to print a class T-shirt designed and printed it themselves, and with their parents' consent sold lemonade to pay for it.

I accompany them and give advice when needed, but I also know how to step aside and let them lead.

If we only believe in our children and let them try, make mistakes, take initiative and dream, they can go so high and so far.

Chapter 21: Do I Allow My Child to Fail?

When my daughter was little, she helped set the table.

She took a glass, and on the way to the table it slipped out of her hands and broke.

For a moment she froze in place and looked at me.

I could have said:

“Leave it, I'll bring the rest of the glasses.”

That way I might have kept another glass from breaking, finished setting the table quickly, and also spared myself a little tension.

But I picked up the pieces myself so that she would not get hurt, and I said to her:

“Oh, what a shame the glass fell. It happens. Wait a moment while I pick this up, and then bring the next glass.”

She hesitated a little, took another glass, and put it on the table.

Just letting her bring the next glass gave her, without words, a clear message:

“I still believe in you.”

In that moment she discovered that one mistake does not take away her chance to try again.

When a child fails, he may get frightened, feel ashamed, or be afraid to try again. If we rush to take the task away from him, under our help he may hear:

“Maybe this is too big for you.”

“Be more careful!” does not help him in a moment like this either. He knows that himself, and more than once he is already blaming himself more than we would blame him. What he needs from us is not another remark, but someone who will help him go on believing in himself even after a mistake.

We meet the fright and the sorrow together with him, and when the place is safe again, we let him come back and try.

This is how a child builds up experiences in which something went wrong, and he recovered, learned, and tried again. Each experience like this adds a little more confidence in himself to his inner store.

Chapter 22: Can I Watch Him Pay a Price?

There is another kind of rescuing.

Sometimes the child is not facing a hard task right now. He is simply not taking care of something that matters to him.

Let us say I see a sticker album he treasures lying on the floor.

I can already imagine what will happen if it stays there.

The album may get torn or lost, and the child will be sorry and hurt.

And right away an urge rises in me to pick it up and put it back in its place.

On the face of it, what is the big deal?

I picked an album up off the floor and saved the child some pain.

But it is worth asking myself:

Who am I helping right now?

The child, or myself?

Maybe I am picking up the album because I cannot bear the thought that it will get lost, and that my child will have to meet that pain.

If I leave it there, the child may indeed pay a price.

But he will also learn something he cannot learn if I always pick up after him:

The things that matter to me are things I need to take care of.

If every time we rush to keep him from the natural result of his actions, how will he learn to take responsibility?

So I can see the album on the floor, and leave it there.

Not out of indifference.

Out of faith.

Faith that my child is able to learn.

That he is able to take responsibility.

And that he is able to cope with mistakes and with their price as well.

And if the album does get lost and the child is sorry, we will not say to him:

“You see? I told you.”

“That's your problem.”

“Now you'll learn.”

We will feel his pain with him.

We will understand his heart.

We will also make room for the pain that rises in us when we see his pain.

But we will not rush to cancel the result for him or rescue him from a price he is able to carry.

Our hand can rest on his shoulder, and our heart can be with him completely.

The album is gone, and what happened cannot be undone.

But it is exactly this painful meeting between his actions and their results that teaches him to take responsibility in a way no explanation can.

How many chips did I give him today?

Chapter 23: The Poker Chip Theory

The idea in the following chapters is based on the “Poker Chip Theory” of the American educator and author Richard (Rick) Lavoie, and is presented here with his permission.

Imagine that every child has a store of invisible poker chips. These chips are the confidence he has built up in himself: the memories of the times he succeeded, overcame something, tried again and discovered that he has strengths.

Every small success can add a chip to the store. A task he managed to finish, a difficulty he did not run away from, a kind word from someone who believes in him, or a responsibility he was given and lived up to: all of these slowly add up to an inner wealth of confidence.

And when a child has many chips, he can afford to risk a few of them. He is willing to raise his hand in class even if he might be wrong, to try something new even if he is not sure he will succeed, to enter a competition, or to take on a hard task. Failing may still hurt, but it does not threaten to wipe out everything the child knows about himself. Behind him there are already many experiences that tell him: I succeeded before, and even if I do not succeed now, something still remains in me.

Lavoie illustrates this with a poker table. Two players sit across from each other: in front of one there is a big pile of chips, and in front of the other only a few. The player who has many can play at ease. He takes risks and places bets, and if he loses once, he still has plenty left.

For the player who has few, every bet is a big risk. So he will play very carefully, hold on to every chip, and sometimes prefer not to play at all.

It is the same with children. What sometimes looks to us like self-confidence, or the opposite, like hesitancy, is not only a trait of the child. To a great extent it is the result of what is in his store.

It is not enough to tell a child, “Believe in yourself.” His confidence is built from the real chips he gathers along the way, and we can give him many chances to gather them.

Chapter 24: What Happens When a Child Doesn't Have Enough Chips?

Now imagine a child who has very few chips left in his store.

This is not always because he really fails more than others. Often the store empties because of the way the people around him meet him: tasks that are not suited to him, comparisons with others, and many words about what he does not do right, from us parents as well. Over time he already expects that the next attempt will also end in disappointment.

Sometimes these are not only remarks about what the child did, but jabs and sweeping statements:

“Oh, you're really something.”
“Come on, can't you see what you're doing?”
“This always happens to you.”

These sentences may slip out in a moment of anger, but the child hears in them much more than criticism of a particular act. He may hear that this is how we see him.

And when his store is almost empty anyway, one jab like that can take much more from it than we think.

In a situation like this, a new attempt does not feel to him like one more small chance. For him it is a bet on the few chips he still has left. If he fails, he is afraid nothing will be left.

A child like this says his stomach hurts or that he does not want to go to school. In Lavoie's words, what he is really saying is: “Mom, please don't make me play this game. I don't have enough chips.” But the game does not stop. He has to go to school, raise his hand, take tests and cope, day after day.

So he may avoid, refuse, react with anger, make light of the task, or announce in advance that he does not care. Sometimes what looks to us like laziness or unwillingness is a child protecting his almost empty store with all his strength.

And sometimes he does exactly the opposite: runs wild, does silly things or takes reckless risks, like a player who bets everything, because he does not have much to lose anyway.

There are also children who look for chips somewhere else: a place where they can feel they belong, strong or successful, without risking the little they have left. It might be, for example, a group that accepts them easily and gives them a sense of status, even if belonging to it means behaving in ways that are not good for them.

In all of these ways the child is responding to the same thing: a store that is almost empty. The avoiding, the running wild or the search for belonging somewhere else is not the problem itself. It is a sign that he is short of chips.

So the solution is not only to stop the behavior, but to help him fill the store again.

It will not help if I give him a speech now about the importance of effort, or just tell him, “You can do it.” He needs to gather real chips: to get a task that is a little above where he stands, but still possible for him; to meet an adult who sees his progress; and to discover from his own experience that effort can lead to success.

This does not mean we should pity him, lower our expectations of him, or do things for him. Pity like that only tells him that we do not believe in him either. He needs real successes, ones he worked hard for and that are truly his.

Richard Lavoie says with a smile: if the child is good at tightening screws, before he comes home, loosen all the screws in the house and give him a screwdriver.

It is an exaggeration, of course, but the idea is clear: we need to look for what the child is good at and create real chances for him to succeed exactly there.

As the store fills up, the child becomes more willing to try. He no longer feels that every mistake proves he is incapable. He has something to lean on, so he can take risks, fail sometimes, and try again.

Chapter 25: Filling the Child's Store

Every person who meets the child can add to his store or take from it. A parent who gives him responsibility and trusts him, a teacher who notices his progress, a friend who picks him for a game, and even a bus driver who greets him with a smile and treats him kindly: each of them can add a chip to his store.

The surroundings have an effect in the other direction too. A friend's mockery, a comparison between siblings, a cutting remark from an adult, or a label that comes back again and again can take from the store.

When an action needs correcting, we can talk about the action without turning the mistake into a definition of the child. Instead of saying, “You can't do it,” we can say, “Here you haven't managed yet. Let's see what will help you do it differently.” This way even meeting a mistake can become an experience that adds strength to him instead of draining him.

From this comes another responsibility of ours as parents: to pay attention to which frameworks our child enters, who the adults are that meet him, and what kind of look he gets there. As far as it is in our hands, we need to make sure he grows up in surroundings that see his strengths, believe in him, and add as many chips as possible to his store.

Our goal is simple: to help the child arrive at the challenges of his life with enough chips in his store. He will need them to raise his hand in class, make connections, try something unfamiliar, cope with rejection, get through a job interview, and one day also to propose marriage.

That is our responsibility as parents: to make sure that every night our child goes to sleep with more chips than he had when he got up in the morning.

Chapter 26: What We Shine a Light On - Grows

Without noticing, we may spend the day talking to the children mainly about what was not okay.

“How many times do I have to ask?”
“Why are you bothering him again?”
“Can't we talk respectfully here?”

We notice right away when the child does not listen, when the siblings bother each other, when the room stays messy, or when the way people talk at home becomes disrespectful. About all of these we make remarks.

But on that same day the children also got along with each other, helped at home, and did quite a few good things without our asking.

These things happened quietly. Everything was fine, so we did not say anything either.

And so it may be that during the day many good things happened at home, and still most of our words went to what was not okay.

But what we shine a light on, grows.

When a child hears us noticing his effort, his consideration, his overcoming and his responsibility, his attention turns to them. He begins to see the good in himself and to understand that this is something people notice and value at home. That raises the chance that he will want to choose it again.

On the other hand, when the child gets most of our attention exactly when he is bothering, arguing or not listening, he learns that this is where our presence is. The very behavior we want to reduce becomes the sure way to win a look, words and a response.

During the day he also hears mainly what is not okay, what he did wrong and what his brother did to him. Little by little he may learn to see himself, his siblings and his home through the mistakes, the fights and the problems.

The effect is not only on the child. It is on the atmosphere of the whole home.

When most of the words heard at home are remarks and corrections, a negative atmosphere forms easily. The remark creates tension, the child gets defensive or argues, the tension brings more unpleasant behavior, and that brings another remark.

Little by little it seems that all we do at home is make remarks. The children may pick up this language too: they notice mainly what the other one did wrong, make remarks to each other and look for someone to blame.

It is possible to create a different movement at home. When we get used to seeing the good and giving it words, it becomes more present too. The child learns to recognize the good strengths inside him, and the home gets a language with more attention to effort, responsibility, consideration and overcoming.

There is no need for exaggerated compliments or artificial excitement. It is enough to describe simply what we saw:

“I noticed you waited for him patiently.”
“I saw it was hard for you, and you still managed.”
“It was nice for me to see how you helped each other.”
“You tidied up without my asking. That's responsibility.”

In that moment the child is not just getting a compliment. We are giving him a mirror in which he can see the good in himself. He discovers that not only his mistakes are seen, but also his good choices and his small efforts.

And what happens when the atmosphere at home is already not good? The children are fighting, bothering each other, and we have to make remarks?

If an action needs to be stopped, we stop it. If a boundary is needed, we set it. But after we have made a remark, we do not stay on alert only for the next negative thing. We also wait for a chance to shine a light on the good.

Sometimes we will only need to wait a few minutes. The child who was bothering his brother will make room for him, hand him something he asked for, manage to stop himself, or this time speak to him kindly.

And when that happens, we can say to him:

“I saw you made room for him.”
“I was glad to see you managed to stop.”
“Now you spoke to him kindly.”

I try to make sure that for every negative remark heard at home there are at least two good ones. Not as a rigid account I have to keep, but as a reminder of where I want to turn my attention.

If I had to make a remark to the child, I will wait for real chances to notice the good he does as well. I will not make up a compliment. I will simply open my eyes, look for the good moments, and remember not to leave them in silence.

The more we look for them, the more we discover that the good was in the home all along. Alongside the fights there are reconciliations. Alongside the not listening there is overcoming. Alongside the mess there are moments of responsibility, generosity and consideration.

It is worth asking ourselves at the end of the day:

Which words were heard more at home today: words of criticism and correction, or words that notice effort and the good?
What atmosphere did I create with my attention?

Our attention is light.

It does not only shine on what is already in the home. It helps decide what will be more present in it tomorrow.

And whatever we choose to shine a light on again and again will get room, grow stronger, and grow.

Part V: The Ability to Say “No”

Does he agree, or is he afraid to disappoint?

Chapter 27: Caution: A Child Who Is Too Good

There is a child who is very easy to raise.

He listens, gives in, helps, tries to do everything he is asked, and almost never says “no.”

It is comfortable to be his parent.

And exactly because of that, it is easy not to notice what is happening inside him.

There are sensitive children who notice every change in a parent's face right away. They feel our disappointment before we have said anything, and they rush to adjust themselves.

Little by little the child learns what Abba wants, what Ima expects, and what will make the teacher happy. He becomes an expert at recognizing what everyone wants, but less sure of what he himself wants and of what is unpleasant for him.

He is gentle, well-behaved and easy with his friends too. Exactly the child we all dream of.

I thought so too. Once I consulted the parenting guide about a difficulty we were having with one of the children. In the middle of it I said, “He's a good boy, really a good boy...”

And she answered:

“Of course he's a good boy. He's a pleaser.”

That sentence shook me. Suddenly I understood that a “good” child is not necessarily a child who is doing well. More than that, this is not the reason I want my child to be good. Not out of a need to please me, but out of what he himself feels and chooses.

Because when this goodness comes from a constant need to keep everyone happy, the child pays a price for it.

He is so busy with what others want from him that he finds it hard to recognize what he himself wants. He knows how to say the right thing, give in and get along, but he is not always sure he is allowed to refuse, express a different opinion, or say that something is unpleasant for him.

Over time he may find it hard to develop an inner backbone. Before he asks himself what he wants, what seems right to him or what does not suit him, he is already busy with how others will react.

Will they be angry or disappointed in him, and will the way they treat him change?

And when fear of other people's reactions runs him, it is hard for him to stand his ground. He may give in, stay quiet, and agree even to things that do not suit him.

A child who does not feel safe saying “no” inside a loving home will find it hard to find the strength to say “no” outside, with his friends, and later also in the relationships he will meet in his adult life.

A pleasing child acts out of one fear: that if he disappoints us, something in our relationship will crack. So the most important thing we can give him is the experience that the relationship holds even when he disappoints us: when he refuses, chooses differently, complains or gets it wrong. Alongside this, we can help him get to know his own inner voice.

Is he really allowed to say “no”?

When I ask my child for a favor, what happens if he refuses?

I am not talking about something he has to do. If he has to, I will tell him so clearly, as a requirement, and expect him to listen. I am talking about a real favor, something I did not require of him to begin with. For example, at a Shabbat meal with guests I asked him to say the dvar Torah he learned, and he answers, “I don't want to.” Or I asked, “Would you agree to give up your room for the guests on Shabbat?” and he answers, “No, I don't want to sleep in the living room.” In a moment like that, is “no” also an answer I am able to accept?

This is our home, and we are allowed to decide that the room is given to the guests. If we decided, we will tell him so clearly. But if we chose to ask him, we need to be ready to hear a negative answer too.

When he does refuse, do I accept his refusal, or do I get offended, show disappointment and put pressure on him until he takes it back and says “yes”?

A pleasing child will usually rush to agree to the request. So it is not enough to be glad that he agrees. It is important to make sure he knows that refusing is also an option, and that it does not make us value him or love him any less.

When he does refuse, we can simply say, “Okay, thank you for telling me.” Without being offended, and without trying to convince him to take it back.

Our joy when he agrees should also be simple. If our warmth grows every time he gives in and agrees, he learns that this is how you win love, and that is exactly what feeds the pleasing.

Letting him choose where the choice belongs to him

It is worth leaving the child choices in the places where they truly belong to him.

Which clothes he likes to wear. What, out of the options available, he prefers to eat. What he wants to play.

That way he gets used to recognizing what he wants, stating a position and standing behind it, without being afraid that every choice different from ours will disappoint us.

Connecting him to what he feels

A pleasing child is used to looking outward, at what others want from him. We can help him look inward too. When he rushes to give in or to help, we can gently ask him, “How do you feel about it?” or “Do you really feel like it, or are you doing it for me?”

Not so that he will stop helping, but so that he gets used to paying attention to his own voice too.

Making room for feelings that are unpleasant for us

With that same child, when he was little, it happened almost every time I came to pick him up from a friend. He was disappointed and wanted to stay, and I would explain to him logically, or so I thought:

“We drove you, we let you have fun, and if you keep complaining, we won't feel like doing it again.”

I was sure I was teaching him gratitude. In that same conversation I told the guide about this too, and she asked me:

“Isn't he allowed to be disappointed?”

That question stopped me in my tracks. He was not ungrateful. He enjoyed himself, wanted to stay, and was disappointed that it was time to go. Instead of making room for that disappointment, I taught him that disappointment endangers what he gets from us.

Today I would say to him:

“You wanted to stay longer, and you're really disappointed that we came to get you.”

We still go home. But the child discovers that he does not need to hide his disappointment to keep us happy.

What happens when the good child behaves badly?

The big test comes when the “good” child suddenly behaves badly.

Sometimes we are much more shocked than the act itself justifies:

“How could you, of all people, do something like that?”

It is not only the act that bothers us. For a moment the child broke the image we got used to seeing in him.

If he hurt someone, was disrespectful or did not listen, I will set a boundary and deal with what he did. But I do not want him to feel that my love for him depends on his going on being the easy child who never disappoints me.

And deep inside, I may even feel a little relief. Not about the act, but about the fact that he allows himself to be with me even when he is unhappy, resists, gets it wrong, or tests a boundary.

He does not have to hold up the image of the perfect child all the time in order to feel secure in his place at home.

The boundary stays a boundary. But it does not turn into a crisis of love.

I want my child to grow up with an inner voice of his own. To know what he wants and what does not suit him, to be able to state a position, say “no,” and protect himself even with people who are important to him.

He can stay a gentle, considerate, good child, without disappearing into what others want.

He begins to build this backbone at home, when he discovers that refusing, disappointment and mistakes do not endanger his place with us.

Chapter 28: Am I Manipulating My Child Emotionally?

Sometimes we want the child to do something, but instead of asking him clearly or setting a boundary, we work on him through our own feelings.

“After everything I've done for you...”
“If you really cared about me, you wouldn't behave like this.”
“Look how much sorrow you're causing Ima.”
“Today we bought you new shoes, and you can't do one little thing for us?”
“Ima worked all day for you, and this is how she's treated?”
“I guess I'm just not important enough to you anymore.”

We tell the child what we are asking of him, but we also wrap the request in guilt. Instead of simply asking or setting a boundary, we work on the child's feelings to get him to do what we want.

This is emotional manipulation.

Sometimes it hides in a small sigh: “Fine, never mind. I'll manage on my own.” And sometimes it is not even said in words: a hurt silence, a cold tone, or a deliberate distance, until the child “gets it” and makes amends.

Many times we do not do this on purpose. We really are hurt, disappointed or sad. But instead of meeting our feeling and carrying it, we put it on the child's shoulders.

Even when the pain is real, behind the words, and behind the silence, the child hears an emotional bill:

I gave to you. I worked hard for you. I loved you. And now you owe me what I want.

What we gave him, the shoes, the rides, the long workday, was given because we are his parents. It is not a down payment on his obedience, and it is not a debt we can collect from him the next time he refuses a request of ours.

When love becomes a tool for pressure

Manipulation shows up in two kinds of situations.

Sometimes it is about something we are entitled to require, and the guilt comes instead of the boundary. Instead of saying clearly “That's not how we behave” and standing by it, we say, “Look how much sorrow you're causing Ima.”

The child may stop. But now he is carrying on his shoulders something much heavier than the act itself: not only did he do something wrong, he is the one making Ima suffer.

A child cannot carry a responsibility like that. He does not know how to fix an adult's sorrow, so the guilt stays with him. Over time it may turn into a general feeling that something in him is not okay.

In other cases it is about something the child does not have to do: to come with us somewhere, to give up a plan he made, to tell us something he does not want to share, or to show us affection the way we expect. Here the guilt turns a request into a hidden demand.

Now the child is not only dealing with our request. He has to choose between what he wants and the fear that he will hurt us, or prove that he does not love us enough.

We told him he is allowed to choose, but we turned one of the options into a heavy emotional price:

If you say “no,” Ima will be hurt. If you choose differently, Abba will be disappointed. If you don't do what I want, maybe it means you don't love me.

This power is especially great with a child. He depends on us, needs our love, and wants to know that we are happy with him. When we use this need to get what we want from him, it is very hard to call it free choice.

When we really want to leave him the choice, we can state our wish openly:

“I would be very happy if you came. But if you don't want to, that's okay.”

Responsibility for the action, not for the parent's feelings

Here it is important to tell two things apart.

The child is responsible for the way he behaves and for the effect his actions have on others. If he insulted someone, hurt someone or spoke disrespectfully, it is right to set a boundary and require him to repair it.

“The way you spoke was not respectful. That's not how we talk.”

Perhaps his words also hurt me. That is natural. But I do not need to use my hurt to make him feel guilty. The boundary stands because it is not allowed to talk that way, not because it is the child's job to make sure I am never hurt or disappointed.

Sometimes we really are hurt. We invested, we expected, and the child refused our request or behaved in a way that pained us. This hurt is real, and there is no need to be ashamed of it or pretend it is not there.

But it is not the child's job to heal it. That is our job: to stop, acknowledge what we are feeling, and give the feeling room, as we saw in the first chapters.

There is no need to hide the hurt either. Children feel when something is not okay, and hiding it only confuses them. We can say briefly and simply what we are feeling, and leave it there.

The difference is subtle, but it is the heart of the matter: sharing like this is meant to tell him what is happening with me, not to hand him the job of fixing my feeling. If I share and wait for him to give in or make it up to me so that I will feel better, that is manipulation again, only gentler. If I share and carry the feeling myself, he learns that the people he loves have feelings too, and that they are able to cope with them.

We can share our feeling without putting it on him. When he refused a request:

“I'm a little disappointed, but I understand you want something different.”

When his behavior hurt us:

“That hurt me to hear.”

I want the child to learn:

“I am responsible for the way I behave, and when I hurt someone I need to take responsibility and repair it.”

But I do not want him to learn:

“I am responsible for Abba's and Ima's feelings, and if they are disappointed by a choice of mine, I have to give it up to calm them down.”

It matters to me that the child is considerate of my feelings, and of course I am happy when he wants to make me happy. That is a natural and beautiful part of a loving relationship.

But I do not want the fear of disappointing me to cancel what he wants, or for him to feel that every time I am not pleased, it is his job to give in and fix my mood.

I want him to learn to be considerate of me out of wanting to and not out of fear, without feeling responsible for my feelings.

The feeling that rose in me is mine, and the responsibility for dealing with it is mine. When I remember this, I can simply tell the child what I am asking of him, without turning his love for me into a tool to get him to agree.

Who does he learn to say “no” from?

Chapter 29: The Seesaw Principle

Sometimes I say to my children:

“It doesn't suit me right now.”

“I don't have the energy.”

“I'm tired and need to rest.”

And not only is that okay with me, I am even glad for the chance to say it to them. Because in these moments they meet something important about the way a healthy relationship works.

This is called “the seesaw principle.”

We can imagine three ways a relationship can work.

In an exploitative relationship, one person is always up and the other always down. One asks, receives and demands, and the other gives himself up again and again to satisfy him.

In a selfish relationship the same thing happens from the other direction. I take care only of myself, and the needs, wishes and boundaries of the person in front of me hardly interest me.

But a healthy relationship is like a seesaw. It is not a relationship in which one person is always up and the other always down. There is room in it for the needs and boundaries of both sides.

Sometimes I am available for the request of the person in front of me, and sometimes not. Sometimes I choose to be considerate of him and give in for his sake, and sometimes I need him to be considerate of me.

Of course, the relationship between a parent and a child is not an equal one. The parental responsibility is ours, and we give our children far more than we ask of them. The child is not supposed to take care of us, carry responsibility for our emotional state, or make sure our needs are met.

But the fact that we are parents does not mean we have to erase ourselves.

We too are allowed to be tired, to want quiet, to not be available at a certain moment, and to say that it does not suit us right now.

Of course, not every need of the child can wait just because we are tired. There are things we are responsible for giving him even when it is hard for us. But there are also many requests that can be put off, and sometimes refused.

When a parent regularly erases his own needs and boundaries, the child may learn that this is what a relationship looks like: whoever loves me is always available to me, always gives in for me, and never says no to me.

He may also learn the opposite message: that when he himself loves another person, he must always give up what he wants so as not to disappoint him.

But when we set a simple, clean boundary, without anger and without making the child responsible for our well-being, he meets a different possibility:

“I love you, and right now I need to rest.”

“Your request matters to me, but right now I'm not available for it.”

“You want me to be with you, and I understand you. In a few minutes I'll be free.”

The seesaw principle does not say that sometimes I take care of the child and sometimes he takes care of me. It says that even within a relationship in which I am the one responsible and the main giver, I do not need to erase myself.

When the child meets a parent at home who knows how to give with love, but also sometimes to say “not now,” he meets an example of a relationship with room for both sides.

He sees that it is possible to love and to set a boundary. It is possible to be considerate of another person without disappearing, and it is possible to hear “no” without taking it as rejection or as a lack of love.

He may take this familiarity with a healthy relationship with him into the relationships he will build outside the home: to respect the boundaries of others, and later also to recognize and protect his own boundaries.

A healthy relationship does not ask one of the sides to disappear.

As a parent, the responsibility stays in my hands, but I also have a place in the relationship.

Chapter 30: Does It Feel Right to Me or Not?

Personal safety begins at home.

We want our children to know how to protect themselves even when we are not next to them. We teach them safety rules and tell them when to move away, say no and ask for help.

But there is another important ability, one that is built long before any encounter with a dangerous situation: the child's ability to feel what feels right to him and what does not, and to know that what he feels has meaning.

This ability is built out of everyday experiences at home, and out of the child's sense that his body belongs to him, that people listen to him when he says something hurts or is unpleasant, and that he has control over his body.

It happens in the simplest moments. When we comb his hair with a lice comb, when we cut his nails, and even when we go for a haircut.

Even when the thing has to be done anyway, we can give the child a sense that he is a partner in what happens to his body. To explain to him in advance what we are about to do, to ask what will help him, and to let him choose within the situation:

“Which hand shall we start with?”
“Tell me if it pulls your hair.”
“Let's take a little break and then go on.”
“How would you like your haircut this time?”

That way he gets used to the idea that his feelings matter, that he is allowed to talk about them, and that he has control over his body. He learns to recognize when something feels good to him, when it does not, and when he wants it to stop.

From here we can widen this listening to encounters with other people too. I do this with children through a simple game: “Okay or not okay?”

Is it okay for Ima to hug me?
Is it okay for my uncle to watch me while I get dressed?
Is it okay for the bus driver to give me a high five?
Is it okay for the teacher to put his hand on my shoulder?
Is it okay for an adult to take me alone to a place where there is no one else?
Is it okay for a friend to tickle me?
Is it okay for the doctor to examine me?
Is it okay for a person I don't know to offer me candy and ask me to go with him?

The game does not always have one right answer, and that is not the main thing either. Children usually recognize the problematic situations right away. I simply want to sharpen this ability to recognize from time to time, and practice with them the very act of stopping and looking:

Does this feel right to me or not?

We can also illustrate this with three circles.

In the center is the child himself.

In the first circle are the people close to him: Abba, Ima, siblings, and other people he feels close to.

In the second circle are people he knows and meets in different settings: a teacher, a madrich, a bus driver, a neighbor or a doctor.

In the third circle are people he does not know.

In each circle we can ask together: what kind of closeness is okay here? What kind of touch is okay? What is okay to talk about? And how far is it okay to trust the person in front of me?

Over time, the questions we ask in the game can become questions the child asks himself:

Does this feel right to me?
Does this feel good to me?
Do I want this to go on?

And if something does not feel right, he can move away, say “no” or “stop,” and tell a safe adult.

Safety rules give the child important instructions. This practice also builds in him an inner ability to know his feelings, listen to them, and know that he has a voice in everything that has to do with his body.

Part VI: Giving Him Room

Chapter 31: I Want It to Come from Him

I do not want my child to do things only because I asked him to.

True, sometimes a child needs to do something simply because Abba or Ima said so. He is a child and we are the parents. There are boundaries at home, and not everything can wait until he feels like it.

But in the long run, I do not want to stand next to him all the time, reminding, convincing and demanding. I want the important things to gradually become his own too.

I want it to come from him.

We have already seen how important it is to help the child feel the good that comes after the act. But sometimes it is possible to go one step further: not only to ask him what he feels, but to let go of the requirement and let him meet the feeling on his own.

Showering is a good example of this.

When the children had grown a little and could already take responsibility for keeping clean, I stopped making them shower. I told them until what time they could go into the shower, and whoever wanted to, showered.

I explained to them that people find it more pleasant to be near someone who keeps himself clean. But mainly, I let them meet the feeling themselves.

When they did not shower and felt, especially in the summer, sweaty and sticky, or had trouble falling asleep, I reflected back to them what they were feeling. I did not say, “You see? I told you,” and I did not turn the moment into a speech about cleanliness.

Sometimes, especially in the winter, they would skip showers. But gradually they began to notice for themselves the connection between showering and how they felt.

They got to know the feeling of a sweaty, sticky body, and on the other hand the pleasant feeling after a shower.

They no longer only heard from me that cleanliness is important. They discovered for themselves why it matters to them.

Today they simply run to shower. Without my reminding, convincing or waging a war around it.

I might have gotten an immediate shower if I had made it a daily duty. But I wanted to achieve something deeper: that the children would want to shower because they themselves recognize the need, and not only because Abba said so.

There are clear boundaries in our home, and there are things I require and stand firm on. But not every value has to become a boundary right away, and not in every area do I have to manage the child from the outside.

Exactly because authority at home is clear, I can choose where to use it and where to leave the responsibility in his hands.

The child knows there are things that Abba and Ima decide, and there are things in which he has to think, choose, and meet the result.

To my mind, this experience teaches more than a speech. A speech teaches the child what Abba thinks. The experience allows him to discover for himself why the thing is good for him.

It takes time. The child chooses, feels the result, and learns to know himself. Little by little the value stops being only something Abba requires of him. It becomes his own too.

And when that happens, I no longer need to stand next to him and demand.

It comes from him.

Chapter 32: My Child Does Not Belong to Me

We are buying our daughter a dress.

She chooses a dress that we do not think is pretty. Maybe we are even a little embarrassed about what people will think when they see her wearing it.

And right away we say:

“We're not buying this one. It isn't pretty.”
“If you want a dress, we'll only agree to the other one.”

Afterward we may even pressure her to wear the dress we liked.

All of this seems completely natural to us. We are the parents, we are paying, and it seems to us that we know what is pretty.

But why, actually?

Who is supposed to decide what she wears?

Us, because we are her parents?

Or her, because this is her taste, even if it is different from ours?

This question is not only about clothes. It touches a deeper question: the way we see our children.

Sometimes we forget that our child, as close and as beloved as he may be, is a person separate from us.

The responsibility to educate him and to decide questions of safety, health, halacha and the values of the home rests on us. But in what does not touch on these, it is not our role to decide for him.

Our child is in our care, but he is not our property.

The limits of our control

But there is another layer here, a deeper one. Even in the places where we do decide for the child, our control is much more limited than it seems to us.

We have enormous influence on him. We set the rules of the home, set boundaries, teach him and guide him. But influence, however great it may be, does not really give us control over him.

We can teach him values, but we cannot force him to love them. We can wake him up for tefillah and seat him in shul, but we cannot make him pray.

We can forbid candy at home, but we do not know what he buys on the way home from school. We can require behavior of him as long as he is in our care, but we cannot control what he will want, think and choose inside, and certainly not what he will choose when he grows up.

This may be one of the hardest things for a parent to accept.

We want so much for the child to be well. Sometimes we see where his choices may lead, and it seems to us that if only he were willing to do exactly what we say, we could spare him mistakes, pain and disappointment.

But we do not have that kind of control.

And the harder it is for us to make peace with this fact, the more we may try to control. To explain again and again, to pressure, to threaten, to stir up guilt, or to decide for him even in places where the choice already belongs to him.

Recognizing that I do not have full control over my child is not giving up my authority. Quite the opposite. It allows me to understand what authority can do, and what it will never be able to do.

I can decide what is allowed and what is not at home.
I can keep him from hurting himself or others.
I can set requirements that suit his age.
I can teach, explain, set a personal example, and stand consistently for values that matter to me.

But what is inside him, and the person he will become, I cannot decide.

As the child grows, the responsibility for his life needs to pass gradually from our hands to his. Not because we have nothing more to say, but because the goal was never for him to stay managed by us.

The goal is for him to grow up to be a person who is able to manage himself.

So as he grows, I need to let him more and more choose and be who he is, even when it is different from what I would choose:

In the clothes he likes.
In the interests that draw him.
In the pace at which he opens up.
In his character.
With time, also in bigger decisions that will gradually become his own life.

My child needs my leadership. He needs protection, boundaries, values and my presence.

But he is not a project I am supposed to complete, and he is not an extension of me meant to fulfill the picture I made for him.

He is a person who was entrusted to me.

My role is not to control him, but to lead him as long as he needs my leadership, and gradually to help him take the lead of his own life into his hands.

He is in my care. But he does not belong to me.

Chapter 33: His Birthday

When one of my children's birthdays was coming up, he asked to celebrate it in the park with his friends.

My first reaction was to try to convince him to celebrate at home. At home everything is more orderly and safer. It is easier to plan, to know what is going to happen, and to make sure everything goes as it should.

But when I checked myself honestly, I understood that the question was not only where he would have more fun.

The question is whether I am able to bear the uncertainty in his choice.

What will happen if the plan does not work out? If the weather changes? If the children do not have as much fun as he hoped? And if in the end he is disappointed by the choice he made?

At home I could control almost every detail. In the park I had to let go.

But it was his birthday.

I could present him with the considerations, help him think, and make sure the choice was possible. After that, I had to let him choose.

Not because I was sure his choice would work out, but because the possibility that it would not work out also belongs to his experience.

As parents, we want to spare our children disappointments. Sometimes we already see in advance what they do not see yet, and it seems to us that the most responsible thing is to decide for them.

But if we take the decision away from them wherever there might be disappointment, we will also take away their chance to choose, to try things out, and to learn to live with the results of their choices.

My role was not to guarantee that the birthday would be perfect.

My role was to help him think, to be next to him, and to let him celebrate his birthday the way he chose.

This is true not only on a birthday. Every time I am able to meet the uncertainty that rises in me, room opens up for the child's choice.

For his choice. For his experience. And if need be, also for his disappointment.

By the way, in the end it was a very successful birthday.

Chapter 34: Two Words: Use Your Judgment

We are used to giving children instructions about almost everything. Where to put things, how to do them, and how much to take.

In the classroom too, the questions start at the very beginning of the year:

“Teacher, where should I keep the page you handed out?”
“Should I copy from the board on two lines or three?”

But two of the very first words I teach my students are:

“Your judgment.”

At first they do not always understand what I want from them. They are used to every question having an answer from the adult. They ask, and the adult is supposed to tell them exactly what to do, so that they do not have to stop and think for themselves. But I want to give them a different message: you already know how to think. Look at the situation, understand what is needed, and decide for yourself.

Finding the solution himself

The same principle is true when a problem comes up. If a student forgot a notebook, for example, I can tell him:

“Find a solution.”

He can ask for a sheet of paper, ask a friend, or think of another option. I am there and can help when needed, but I do not rush to think for him.

And even when the child comes and tells me that a friend is bothering him, I do not have to rush to give him a solution. First I will share his anger or his hurt and reflect back to him what he is feeling. After that I can ask:

“What do you think is worth doing?”
“How do you think this could be solved?”

That way the child is not left alone with the difficulty, but he also does not hand me the responsibility for dealing with it right away. I am next to him, and he learns to think, suggest ways, and choose how to act.

At home too I use these two words a lot.

The child comes home on a rainy day and asks:

“Where should I put my wet shoes?”

Instead of telling him right away where to put them, I can answer:

“Your judgment.”

He already knows it is not a good idea to put them where they will dirty the floor or get in the way. Now he needs to look around, think, and find a suitable place himself.

And sometimes I stretch it a little.

“Abba, how many wafers should I take?”
“Your judgment.”

That does not mean I gave him permission to take the whole package. I trust him to look at how many there are, think about the others too, and decide how many is reasonable to take.

In a home where until now there was a rule for every move, an answer like this may really turn into a big story. The child is not yet used to being expected to think. He is only used to hearing how much he is allowed.

But in a home where the children practice using their judgment again and again, it is no longer a dramatic moment. The child stops, thinks and takes. And even if this time he took a little more than I would have chosen, no disaster happened. That is exactly how his judgment is built and refined.

When we tell a child everything, he does not need to think. He only needs to obey. He may know very well what Abba and Ima allow, but not know what is right to do when no one is standing next to him telling him.

There are clear boundaries at home, and where an instruction is needed, I give it. But within these boundaries there is a great deal of space in which the child can look around, take into account the reality and the needs of others, and make a decision himself.

There will not always be a parent or a teacher next to him to tell him what to do. Otherwise, how can we expect him to know how to think and decide when he is on his own?

Judgment does not appear suddenly when the child grows up. It is built in all those small decisions in which we could have answered for him, and chose to let him think.

Chapter 35: Not Every Dynamic Calls for Stepping In

The children are playing together on the computer, each in turn. When it is the little girl's turn, she gives it up and lets her big brother keep going, and not for the first time.

I see that she is calm, standing next to him, still part of the game and even enjoying it. Even so, something in me tightens.

Why is she giving it to him? Why isn't she standing up for herself? As I see it, now it is her turn to play.

A wish rises in me to step in right away:

“Now it's her turn. Give it back to her.”

But I stop myself and understand that this is their dynamic right now. She is not asking me for help, she does not look hurt, and maybe she even enjoys giving it to him.

Not everything that bothers me as a parent requires me to stop it.

Children run a whole world between themselves. They give and take, insist and give in, lead and join. Things do not always go the way I would run them, and that is okay.

If I step in right away and arrange every situation for them, I will not let them run their relationship themselves. More than that, I may bring into it a meaning that was not there at all: that she was supposed to be hurt, that he took over, and that she needs me to protect her.

So I can simply watch and let the game go on.

And if I want to understand better, I can ask her later, calmly:

“I saw you let your brother play instead of you. How was that for you?”

This is a question that leaves the experience in her hands. Maybe she will say it did not bother her at all. Maybe I will discover that she wanted to keep playing but did not know how to tell him. Only after I hear her will I know whether she needs help.

My role is not to manage every dynamic happening around the child for him. I stay close, pay attention and am available to him, but I let him run his relationships, sense what suits him, and discover for himself when he wants to go on and when he needs help.

It is exactly when I do not step into the picture right away that the child gets room to run his own dynamic, meet his strengths, and get along in his own way.

Chapter 36: We Need You at Home

It is worth giving every child at home a regular job.

The first reason is simple: the child is part of the family, and it is natural for him too to take a small part in the life of the home.

But the job is not meant only as help around the house.

It has a deeper meaning.

A child who gets a regular job gets a message:

We need you here.
There are things at home that you are responsible for too.

So when we choose a job for a child, we do not only ask which work we would like to take off our own hands. We also need to ask what suits the child, where he can succeed, what responsibility he is able to carry, and where he can feel that his contribution is real.

One child can be responsible for setting the table. Another for taking out the trash. One can tidy the books, and another can help a little brother get ready.

The job does not need to be big. It needs to suit his age and ability and let him really take part in the life of the home.

It is worth giving a job even to a very young child, as long as it is a job he can really do: putting napkins on the table, moving clothes to the laundry basket, or putting toys back in the box.

Even when his contribution does not yet save us any work, and sometimes even takes time and patience from us, the participation itself gives him an experience of ability, contribution and belonging.

That way, from a young age he experiences:

I take part too.
I am able to contribute too.
This home needs me too.

As the child grows, the job can grow with him. The responsibility widens according to his age, his abilities and where he is. Not to weigh him down, but to let him discover that he is able to carry more.

When a child discovers that people trust him and that there is something real in his care, he does not just get one more chore.

He gets a place.

We are used to thinking that a child feels loved when everything is done for him. But a child needs not only to feel taken care of. He also needs to feel that he is able to give, to contribute, and to matter to others.

A job at home is not only work we put on the child, and it is not only a way to make things easier for ourselves.

It is our way of telling him:

You belong here.
We trust you.
We need you.

Chapter 37: No Phone, Just Us and Them

Sometimes a quarter of an hour a day without a phone is enough: just us and them.

No messages popping up in the middle. No quick glance at the screen. No doing one more thing on the side that just needs finishing.

We are with our children for many hours, but we are not always truly available to them. We cook, tidy the house, answer messages and take care of countless important things. The child is next to us, and we are with him, but our attention is somewhere else.

Children also need a different kind of time.

Moments when Abba or Ima look them in the eyes, really listen to them, and take an interest in their world. Not rushing to teach, correct or get things done. Simply being there.

There is no need to always prepare a special activity. We can sit and talk, play together, go for a short walk, or listen to a story the child wants to tell, even if it is not especially fascinating to us.

The point is not the quarter of an hour as such. What matters is that during this time, long or short, the child feels that we are completely with him.

We give our children so much: clothes, food, classes, games, presents and trips. All of these are important and bring joy.

But sometimes what the child is asking of us is not one more thing we will buy or organize for him.

He is asking for us.

For the look in our eyes.

For our listening.

For the feeling that for one moment, nothing matters to us more than him.

This is one of the most precious gifts we can give our children.

And it costs nothing.

Just put the phone aside, and be with them.

Chapter 38: “Abba, Buy Me…”

We walk into a store with the children, and almost right away it starts:

“Abba, buy me this.”
“Ima, I want one of these.”

Our answer is ready before the request is even finished:

“No.”
“Forget it.”

The child insists, “But why not?” and we get firmer: “I said no.” Within a few moments, a simple shopping trip turns into a confrontation.

When my children were little, we had a different way.

Instead of turning every request into a war, we turned it into a shared experience of excitement and imagination. Instead of getting stressed by what they wanted and trying to put it out, I joined them for a moment.

When the child would point at a toy and say:

“I want this!”

I would smile and say:

“Great that you showed me. Now I know what you might like to get for your birthday or on another occasion.”

I did not promise to buy it.

I did not negotiate.

But I also did not rush to put out his excitement.

Sometimes the children would pick more and more toys they might like to get for their birthday, and I would go along with their excitement. They would gather a few toys they loved, put them together and imagine they were already theirs. I would take a picture of them next to the pile, so that we could remember what they liked, and afterward we would put everything back in its place.

They knew we were not buying the toys now. And still they had room to enjoy them for a moment, to imagine and to dream.

Sometimes the child is not only asking for the object. He is asking us to see his excitement, to step into the dream with him for a moment, and not to turn every wish that rises in him into something that needs to be silenced.

I tried to remember their requests, and there were also special occasions when I did buy them one of the toys they picked. But usually, when the birthday came, they already wanted something completely different.

The wish in the store was real, but it belonged to that moment. They saw, got excited, imagined, and then moved on.

There is no need to convince the child that the toy is not nice, that he does not need it, or that he already has enough toys at home. We can understand that he wants it, be with him inside the excitement, and still not buy it.

Not every request needs to turn into a struggle, and not every wish of the child needs to come true or be put out.

We can simply join his excitement: look at the toy with him, listen to what he liked about it, and maybe even take a picture of him with it.

The toy stays in the store.

But the child's wish got a place.

Part VII: The Parent as a Person

Chapter 39: Taking Care of Ourselves Is a Parental Responsibility

We give our children a great deal.

Time, strength, money, hours of sleep. We give up many things for them, and usually we do it gladly. That is parenthood.

But there is a moment when devotion turns into resentment. And when a parent is resentful, the children pay the price too.

Sometimes it comes out in sentences like:

“Enough already! All day long I do nothing but take care of you. I don't have a life of my own!”

Or:

“After everything I do for you, you can't do this one little thing for me?”

We have already seen what may come about when we lean on disappointment and guilt instead of asking openly. Here we will see where it sometimes comes from: a parent who gave so much that he has no strength left, and now his resentment comes out on the child.

The child did not ask us to erase ourselves, and he also should not have to pay the price of that erasing afterward.

So taking time for ourselves is not selfishness. It is a parental responsibility.

Sometimes we need a few minutes of quiet. A coffee. Music. A short rest, or getting out of the house. Not because the children are not important to us, but exactly because they are very important to us.

Because when we are drained, it does not stay only as resentment. We become impatient, react sharply to every little request, and in the end take out on them a weariness they did not create.

So sometimes I tell the children:

“Abba is recharging his battery now. I'm resting a little so I can come back to you calmer and more patient.”

And that is exactly what I do.

I do not wait until I am completely angry and exhausted. I recognize that I am starting to run down, stop for a while, and then come back to them calmer and more patient.

To be devoted parents, we do not need to cancel ourselves. Quite the opposite: when we also look after our own strength, we are able to give our children much more.

Of course, it is not always possible to stop. There are moments when the child needs us now, and we put our own needs aside.

But giving in once in a while does not need to turn into a whole life in which we have no place.

The time out we take for ourselves is not a break from parenting. It is part of it.

We make sure we have something to give from.

So go make yourself a coffee.

Chapter 40: Sleeping for the Children

It has happened to me a few times that I got angry at the children or reacted sharply to them, and only afterward remembered:

Wait. I went to sleep late last night. I'm just tired.

Sometimes we look for deep explanations for what happened to us. We try to understand what set us off, why a certain behavior of the child made us so angry, and what feeling it stirred up in us.

But sometimes the explanation is much simpler:

We did not sleep enough. Or we have not eaten for hours.

When we are tired or hungry, our patience gets shorter. It is harder for us to bear frustration and stay calm. The same behavior of the child that on another day would hardly bother us suddenly manages to make us lose it.

In these moments, it is not necessarily the child's behavior that changed. Many times what changed is our own state: we are tired or hungry, so we meet the same behavior with much less patience.

From the moment we become parents, sleeping and eating are no longer only our private business.

If I choose to stay up late, I may not be the only one who pays the price of tiredness the next day. My children will also meet an impatient father who gets annoyed at every little thing and reacts strongly to trivial matters.

And when I am hungry, my patience gets even shorter. My responsibility is not to wait until the hunger comes out on them.

I make sure to sleep and eat on time not only for myself, but also so that I can be more patient and calm with my children.

Of course, it does not always depend on us. There are nights when a baby cries or a child is not feeling well. Sometimes we get up after a short night and have no way of catching up on the missing sleep right away.

But even then I can at least be aware of it and prepared for it, and be more careful in my reactions.

On a day like that I will remind myself that although the child's behavior annoys me, the strength of my anger right now comes to a great extent from lack of sleep or from hunger.

My goal today is simply to get through the day in peace: to make fewer remarks about things that bother me, and certainly not to shout.

I already know that after I sleep, I will feel completely different about exactly the same things.

Right now I just need to make it to the evening and catch up on sleep.

Children too, just like us, find it harder to regulate themselves when they are tired or hungry.

Before we look for an educational explanation for an outburst, impatience, or behavior that is not typical of the child, it is worth checking first:

Did he sleep enough?

When did he last eat?

Sometimes we try to educate a child who in that moment simply has no strength to hold himself together. He is running wild, fighting with his siblings, arguing and not listening, and we are already looking for which boundary to set and what we need to teach him.

But no educational tool can replace food and sleep.

A tired or hungry child finds it harder to hold back, listen and bear frustration. He needs us to understand his state and adjust our expectations to the strength he has in that moment.

That does not mean he is allowed to hurt others. If he needs to be stopped, we will stop him. But we will do it with the understanding that right now he may simply have run out of strength.

One of the most basic things in raising children is making sure the child eats and sleeps well. Otherwise, how can we expect him to hold back, listen and control himself?

Just as we adjust our expectations to the child's strength, we need to remember that our own strength is affected by sleep and food too.

Part of our parental responsibility is to take care of ourselves as well: to sleep and eat, so that we will have the strength to educate the children, be with them patiently, and not take our tiredness and hunger out on them.

Sometimes, the most important thing we can do for our children is simply to eat on time and go to sleep.

Chapter 41: Abba Asks for Forgiveness

I went to sleep late, got up tired, and at some point I blew up at the child.

After I calmed down, I understood that my reaction was harsh and unjustified. Now the question was left:

What do I do with this?

Some parents find it hard to ask their children for forgiveness. They are afraid that if they admit they were wrong, the child will see them as weak, use the admission against them, or think that from now on he does not need to listen to them.

I think the opposite.

A parent who is able to stand in front of the child and say honestly, “I was wrong,” does not lose his authority. He shows strength.

It is easier to explain why we blew up.

It is easier to say that the child made us lose it.

It is much harder to look at what we did without running away, without blaming, and without trying to justify ourselves.

We can go to the child and say:

“Before, I shouted at you. I was very tired, but that is not your fault and I should not have taken my tiredness out on you. I'm sorry, and I ask you to forgive me.”

Tiredness can explain what happened, but it does not free me from responsibility. So I will not say:

“I'm sorry I shouted, but you really got on my nerves too.”

The moment I added “but” and handed the responsibility back to the child, I almost canceled the apology.

That does not mean I take back the boundary I set. The child may really have done something that is not allowed, and we may still need to deal with it.

We can say:

“I did not allow what you did, and that boundary stays. But I could have stopped you without shouting and without talking to you the way I did. For the way I reacted, I ask you to forgive me.”

These are two different things.

The child is responsible for his action.

And I am responsible for my reaction.

Asking forgiveness does not make the child right and me wrong about everything that happened. It only says that even when I am the parent, and even when my boundary is justified, I am not exempt from checking myself and repairing what I did wrong.

And there is no need to turn the apology into a long speech. The child does not need to hear our whole inner accounting, and he certainly does not need to reassure us and say that we are excellent parents.

A simple, direct apology is enough:

What I did.

Why it was not right.

And what I will try to do differently next time.

“I shouted at you, and I shouldn't have shouted. Next time, when I feel tired and angry, I'll try to stop before I speak. I'm sorry.”

The child may still be hurt. He may not want to talk or forgive right away. Asking forgiveness is not a way to get quick confirmation from him that everything is fine. We did our part because we needed to take responsibility, and we give him his own time as well.

When a child sees his father ask for forgiveness, he does not learn that Abba is weak.

He learns that a strong person does not have to pretend he is always right.

He learns that a mistake does not have to end in blaming, hiding or distance. It is possible to acknowledge it, be sorry, and try to repair it.

And he learns that even in a close relationship there are sometimes hurts, but it is possible to come back to them, talk about them, and rebuild the closeness.

We ask our children to say sorry when they hurt someone. It is hard to expect them to do it sincerely if they have never seen us do it ourselves.

The child does not need a father who is never wrong.

He needs a father who, when he is wrong, has the strength to admit it, ask for forgiveness, and repair it.

Chapter 42: Parents Make Mistakes Too

We asked the child for forgiveness.

We admitted we were wrong, took responsibility, and thought about how we could act differently next time.

And now we also need to know how to move on.

Sometimes we find this hard. The incident is already over, the child went back to playing and maybe even forgot about it, but we are still holding a trial against ourselves:

“What kind of parent am I?”

“How could I talk to him like that?”

“Maybe I damaged him?”

“After everything I learn and write, I failed again.”

There is room for this pain. It is good that we feel uncomfortable after we have hurt the child. The feeling of regret helps us stop, take responsibility and try to repair.

But there is a difference between taking responsibility and beating ourselves up.

Taking responsibility says:

I was wrong. I'm sorry. I will repair what I can and try to learn from it.

Beating ourselves up says:

I was wrong, so I am a bad father.

And when we turn every mistake into a verdict on our whole parenthood, we do not become better parents. We only sink into guilt, helplessness and fear of the next mistake.

It is worth stopping to ask ourselves how we would respond if another parent, someone we know and love, told us about exactly the same incident.

He would say:

“I was tired. I lost my patience and shouted at my child. Afterward I was so sorry.”

Would we say to him:

“You're a terrible father. You've probably ruined your child”?

Of course not.

We would say to him:

“You're human. You were tired and you slipped. It happens to parents. It's good that you noticed, and good that you went back to your child and asked for forgiveness. Now try to learn from it and move on.”

We are allowed to give ourselves the same understanding.

Self-compassion does not blur the mistake. It does not say that every reaction of ours is acceptable and that there is no need to change. Quite the opposite: we look honestly at what we did, but we do not crush ourselves because of it.

We say to ourselves:

“I didn't want to react that way, and I understand why it happened. I was tired, overwhelmed or out of strength. I'm sorry for what I did, and next time I'll try to stop earlier. And still, this mistake does not define my whole parenthood.”

Sometimes it seems to us that our children need parents who will never make a mistake. That every shout, every unnecessary sentence or wrong decision might shake them and stay with them for years.

There is no doubt that our actions have an effect. If a hurt happens again and again and we refuse to see it or repair it, it may harm the child and the relationship.

But not every parenting mistake is a disaster.

Here too, we can believe in the child's strengths.

A child who grows up in a relationship founded on love, stability, presence and values does not break from every mistake of ours. He is able to cope even with moments when Abba and Ima are not at their best.

More than that: when he sees that we were wrong, came back, asked for forgiveness and repaired it, he learns something true about relationships. He discovers that a good relationship is not one in which no one ever hurts or fails. A good relationship is one in which it is possible to recognize the hurt, take responsibility for it, and come close again.

Trusting the child does not free us from responsibility. It only reminds us that we are not holding in our hands a vessel so fragile that every small mistake will shatter it.

He has strengths.

Our relationship has strengths.

And we too have the ability to repair.

Parenthood is not a test in which every mistake lowers our grade. It is a long road on which we try, succeed, fail, learn and try again.

The goal is not to be a perfect parent.

The goal is to be a parent who is willing to see himself honestly, take responsibility when needed, and treat himself with humanity as well.

When we have failed, we will ask for forgiveness.

We will learn from the mistake.

And then we will forgive ourselves too, and go on being our children's parents.

Chapter 43: Do I Love My Child, or the Child I Hope He Will Be?

Let us try a small exercise.

Close your eyes and think about the thing that is hardest for you in your child. It might be outbursts, being overweight, shyness, difficulty at school, or anything else.

Now imagine that it does not pass. Not in a month, not in a year, and maybe never.

What is happening in your heart right now? Are you still able to love him just as much?

This is a very hard question, because every parent has a dream of who his child will be. We want him to be well, to succeed, to overcome his difficulties and fulfill the strengths hidden in him. But sometimes, without noticing, we find it hard to accept the child standing in front of us because we are too busy with the child we hope he will be.

The child feels it. He feels when we are happy with him as he is, and when we are waiting for him to change so that we can finally be satisfied with him. Even if we do not say it outright, he may sense that our full love is somewhere further down the road: after he calms down, succeeds, loses weight, overcomes it, or becomes more like the child we imagined.

And the great challenge is to keep loving the child standing in front of us, even when reality does not fulfill our dream.

It is hard. But he deserves it, simply because he is our child.

This is unconditional love.

Unconditional love does not mean giving up on growth. It is allowed to set boundaries, it is allowed to teach, and it is allowed to want things to be better for him. But the child does not need to change for us to love him.

He needs to know that even while we are helping him grow, we are not waiting for him to turn into a different child. We love him now. Exactly as he is.

The child needs to know, and more than that, to feel:

Even if you stay this way for a long time, you are loved.

This may be the greatest gift a parent can give his child.

Some time ago, after several days in which I went deep into the idea of unconditional love and did some inner work, I was driving with one of my children. In the middle of the drive, for no special reason, I said to him:

“I love you so much. I love you just like this, exactly the way you are.”

I really meant it, and a shiver went through me.

Even now, as I write these lines, tears come to my eyes.

It was the simplest moment there is, just like that, in the middle of a drive. But it was also a great and pure moment. A moment of pure, true love, flowing from a father's heart to a son's heart. A moment that comes once in several years. And maybe... maybe a moment that comes once in a lifetime.
